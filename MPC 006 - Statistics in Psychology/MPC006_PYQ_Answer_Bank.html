PYQ Answer Bank — MPC-006 (Statistics in Psychology)

Organised in the same sequence as the PYQ Analysis. Use each framework as an exam answer: definition → core logic/assumptions → procedure or applications → interpretation → conclusion. Adjust the length to the mark band.

1. Data, classification, frequency & descriptive statistics

Organising Data and Descriptive Statistics 10 MARKS

Use for data classification, frequency distribution, measures of central tendency/dispersion and graphical presentation.

  1. Meaning: Statistics begins by converting raw observations into an ordered, intelligible description. Descriptive statistics summarise what the obtained data show; they do not by themselves generalise beyond the data.
  2. Classification: data may be qualitative or quantitative, discrete or continuous, and organised into categories or class intervals. The classification must be mutually exclusive and exhaustive.
  3. Frequency distribution: table scores and their frequencies; relative and cumulative frequencies assist comparison. Tables should have a clear title, categories and totals.
  4. Central tendency: mean uses all scores, median locates the middle score, and mode identifies the most frequent score. Choice depends on scale, skew and outliers.
  5. Dispersion: range, quartile deviation, variance and standard deviation show how spread out scores are. A mean without dispersion can conceal major individual differences.
  6. Display: bar diagrams suit categories; histograms/frequency polygons suit continuous distributions; graphs reveal form, concentration and possible skew.

Conclusion: A sound description reports both a typical value and variability, then selects tables/graphs that preserve the nature of the data.

2. Parametric, non-parametric & measurement

Parametric and Non-parametric Statistics 10 MARKS

  1. Parametric procedures are generally used with interval/ratio data when assumptions such as suitable distributional form, independence and appropriate variance conditions are met. Examples include t, Pearson r and ANOVA.
  2. Non-parametric procedures make fewer distributional assumptions and commonly use nominal data, ordinal ranks or frequency data. Examples are Mann–Whitney U, Wilcoxon, Kruskal–Wallis, chi-square and Kendall tau.
  3. Measurement matters: nominal scores classify; ordinal scores rank; interval scores have equal units but no true zero; ratio scores have equal units and a true zero.
  4. Choice: identify scale, sample relation (independent/paired), number of groups and the question—difference, association or frequencies—before selecting the test.
  5. Merit and limit: parametric tests can be efficient when assumptions are defensible; non-parametric tests are useful when those conditions are unsuitable but may use less numerical information.

Conclusion: Non-parametric does not mean inferior. It is the appropriate route when the data and design require it.

3. Hypothesis testing, significance & errors

Hypothesis Testing and Errors of Decision 10 MARKS

  1. Hypothesis: a testable statement about a population. The null hypothesis (H₀) ordinarily states no difference/association; the alternative (H₁) states the research possibility.
  2. Set α: the level of significance (often .05) is the tolerated probability of rejecting a true H₀. A one-tailed test predicts direction; a two-tailed test allows either direction.
  3. Test statistic: obtain the appropriate value, use degrees of freedom where required, and compare it with the critical region or probability criterion.
  4. Decision: reject H₀ when the result falls in the critical region; otherwise retain/fail to reject H₀. This is a probability decision, not proof that H₀ is true.
  5. Errors: Type I error rejects a true H₀; Type II error fails to reject a false H₀. Lowering α reduces Type I risk but can increase Type II risk unless power/sample size improve.

4. Distributional foundations

Normal Probability Curve, Skewness and Kurtosis 10 MARKS

  1. Normal probability curve: a symmetrical, bell-shaped theoretical distribution. Mean, median and mode coincide; total area is one; tails approach but do not touch the baseline.
  2. Use: it provides a reference for standard scores, probability statements, sampling distributions and many parametric procedures.
  3. Skewness: indicates asymmetry. Positive skew has a longer right tail; negative skew has a longer left tail. Mean, median and mode separate accordingly.
  4. Kurtosis: describes relative peakedness/tail form compared with the normal curve: leptokurtic, mesokurtic and platykurtic forms.
  5. Implication: marked divergence, extreme outliers or unsuitable measurement can affect the defensibility and interpretation of some parametric analyses.

Standard Error and Significance of Difference Between Means 6 MARKS

Standard error is the standard deviation of a sampling distribution. It indicates the expected sampling fluctuation of a statistic; as sample size rises, the standard error generally becomes smaller. In comparing means, the observed difference is evaluated relative to its standard error. A large difference relative to expected sampling fluctuation supports rejection of H₀, subject to the test’s assumptions and decision rule.

5. Correlation and regression

Pearson Product-Moment Correlation 10 MARKS

  1. Meaning: Pearson’s r describes the direction and degree of a linear relationship between two continuous variables.
  2. Range: r lies from −1 to +1. The sign gives direction; magnitude gives strength. r = 0 indicates no linear association, not necessarily no relation of any kind.
  3. Procedure: inspect a scatterplot, check the variables/design and calculate the coefficient from paired scores. Outliers and restricted range can distort r.
  4. Interpretation: report direction, degree and the variables in words. The coefficient of determination, r², describes the proportion of variance accounted for in the linear model.
  5. Limitation: correlation does not establish causation; a third variable and direction of influence remain possible.

Special Correlation Coefficients 10 MARKS

  • Spearman rho: association between ranks/ordinal data.
  • Kendall tau: rank association, useful where concordant and discordant ordering is considered.
  • Phi: association between two genuinely dichotomous variables.
  • Point-biserial: one continuous variable and one true dichotomy.
  • Rule: name the form of both variables before choosing the coefficient; do not apply Pearson mechanically to every association.

Partial, Part, Multiple Correlation and Regression 10 MARKS

  1. Partial correlation estimates the association of X and Y after controlling a specified variable from both.
  2. Part (semi-partial) correlation removes the control variable from one of the variables, showing its unique contribution to the criterion.
  3. Multiple correlation concerns the relationship between a criterion and a set of predictors.
  4. Regression produces an equation to predict a criterion score from predictor score(s). In bivariate regression, the line expresses the predicted Y for a given X; multiple regression uses more than one predictor.
  5. Caution: controlling a variable statistically does not itself prove a causal explanation.

6. Comparing means: t and ANOVA

t Test for Difference Between Means 10 MARKS

  1. Purpose: a t test evaluates whether a mean difference is large relative to expected sampling error.
  2. Design: independent-samples t compares separate groups; paired-samples t compares related observations such as before–after scores.
  3. Procedure: state H₀/H₁, calculate the appropriate t and degrees of freedom, set α, compare with the critical value and interpret in the variables’ terms.
  4. Conditions: choose it when measurement/design and assumptions make a parametric comparison appropriate; otherwise consider a rank alternative.

One-way Analysis of Variance 10 MARKS

  1. Purpose: one-way ANOVA compares three or more means defined by one factor, avoiding repeated t-test inflation of Type I error.
  2. Logic: it partitions variability into between-group variation and within-group variation. F is their ratio: a relatively large F suggests group means differ beyond expected error.
  3. Steps: state hypotheses; identify factor and levels; obtain sums of squares, degrees of freedom and mean squares; compute F; compare it with the critical value.
  4. Interpretation: a significant F says at least one mean differs; it does not identify which means differ without appropriate follow-up comparisons.
  5. Assumptions: independent observations, suitable score scale/distribution and appropriate variance conditions.

Two-way ANOVA and Interaction 10 MARKS

Two-way ANOVA examines two factors simultaneously. It evaluates a main effect for each factor and an interaction: whether the effect of one factor depends on the level of the other. Define factors, levels and cells, then interpret each effect separately. A significant interaction should be explained in terms of the particular pattern of cell means, not as a vague statement that “both factors matter.”

7. Non-parametric tests

Mann–Whitney U Test 10 MARKS

The Mann–Whitney U test compares two independent groups using ranks. Combine scores, rank them, obtain rank sums and calculate U; compare the obtained value with the relevant critical value or probability. It is appropriate for ordinal/ranked data or when an independent-groups parametric comparison is unsuitable. State that it compares group distributions/ranks rather than claiming it directly compares arithmetic means.

Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test 6 MARKS

The Wilcoxon signed-rank test is for two related measurements. Find paired differences, omit/handle zero differences as required, rank absolute differences, restore signs and compare signed ranks. It is a rank alternative when paired data do not support the corresponding parametric comparison.

Kruskal–Wallis Test 6 MARKS

Kruskal–Wallis compares three or more independent groups through ranks. Pool and rank observations, obtain group rank totals and calculate the test statistic. It is the non-parametric alternative for an independent-groups one-factor comparison. A significant result indicates that group distributions are not all alike and may require follow-up examination.

Chi-square Test 10 MARKS

  1. Data: chi-square works with observed frequencies in nominal categories.
  2. Logic: compare observed frequencies (O) with frequencies expected (E) under H₀; the statistic sums the discrepancy relative to expectation.
  3. Forms: goodness-of-fit examines whether one distribution matches an expected pattern; independence examines whether categorical variables are associated.
  4. Interpretation: a significant value indicates a departure from the expected frequency pattern. State the categories and direction/pattern; do not report it as a mean difference.
  5. Care: categories should be mutually exclusive and expected-frequency conditions should be considered.

8. Interpretation and reporting

How to Report a Statistical Conclusion 6 MARKS

  1. Name the design and statistic, and state why it fitted the data.
  2. State H₀/H₁ and the significance criterion.
  3. Report the obtained statistic and relevant degrees of freedom or coefficient.
  4. Give the decision on H₀ and translate it to the psychological variables.
  5. State one appropriate boundary: correlation is not causation, a significant ANOVA needs follow-up, or a result depends on assumptions and sample context.
