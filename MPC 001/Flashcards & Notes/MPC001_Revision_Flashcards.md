### Flashcard 1 — Cognitive Psychology: Meaning, History, Domains

**FRONT:** Define cognitive psychology. Outline its history from ancient philosophy to the modern neocognitive revolution. List the twelve principal domains.

**BACK:**

**Definition:** "The study of how people perceive, learn, remember, and think about information" (Neisser, 1967). Also: "the scientific study of the mind." William James (1890): "Mind and world have evolved together, and in consequence are something of a mutual fit."

**History — three periods:**
1. *Early thoughts:* Empiricists (knowledge from experience) vs. nativists (innate). Aristotle — knowledge in heart; Plato — in brain.
2. *Renaissance & beyond:* Mundus sensibilis (senses) + mundus intellectualis (divine). British Empiricists (Berkeley, Hume, J. Mill, J.S. Mill) — three types of internal representation. 19th century psychologists (Wundt, Titchener vs. Brentano: structure vs. process). William James — *Principles of Psychology* (1890).
3. *20th century:* Behaviourism (S-R) displaced mental-process study for ~50 years. Tolman (1932) — cognitive map. Bartlett (1932) — schema. Neocognitive revolution (1950s): six forces — (1) failure of behaviourism, (2) communication theory, (3) modern linguistics, (4) memory research, (5) computer science/AI, (6) cognitive development (Piaget).

**Twelve domains:** Cognitive Neuroscience, Perception, Pattern Recognition, Attention, Consciousness, Memory, Knowledge Representation, Imagery, Language, Developmental Psychology, Thinking & Concept Formation, Human & Artificial Intelligence.

**Key terms for marks:** scientific study of the mind; empiricists vs. nativists; William James (1890); Tolman (1932) cognitive map; Bartlett (1932) schema; six forces of neocognitive revolution; twelve domains; behaviourism; Gestalt isomorphism.

### Flashcard 2 — Research Methods in Cognitive Psychology

**FRONT:** What are the four distinctive research methods used in cognitive psychology? Describe each and note its advantages and limitations.

**BACK:**

**Goals of research:** data gathering, data analysis, theory development, hypothesis formulation, hypothesis testing, application. Seek to "explain cognition as well as describe it."

**Four methods:**
1. **Laboratory/controlled experiments:** IV/DV/control variables; random, representative sample; infer *probable causality*. Advantages: control, causality. Limitations: artificiality.
2. **Psychobiological research:** (a) post-mortem brain study, (b) brain images of individuals with known cognitive deficits, (c) brain imaging during normal cognitive activity (MRI, PET, EEG). Advantages: direct neurological evidence. Limitations: correlational, ethical constraints.
3. **Self-reports, case studies, naturalistic observation:** self-reports (individual's own account), case studies (in-depth), naturalistic observation (everyday, non-lab). Useful for hypothesis *formulation* and rare events. Experiments better for hypothesis *testing*.
4. **Computer simulations & AI:** indirect — computer-based models of cognition; direct — simulations and AI. Some model "the entire cognitive architecture of the human mind."

**Cognitive science** = cross-disciplinary field using cognitive psychology, psychobiology, AI, philosophy, linguistics, anthropology.

**Key terms for marks:** independent/dependent/control variables; probable causality; psychobiological research (3 categories); self-reports, case studies, naturalistic observation; computer simulations and AI; cognitive science as cross-disciplinary.

### Flashcard 3 — Cognitive Neuroscience

**FRONT:** What is cognitive neuroscience? How has the relationship between cognitive psychology and neuroscience evolved? What imaging techniques are used?

**BACK:**

Cognitive neuroscience is the convergence of cognitive psychology, computer science, and neuroscience. Every cognitive process from sensation to memory is supported by electrochemical processes in the brain and nervous system.

**Evolution:** Early — little attention to physiological psychology. Karl Lashley (1929) debated localisation vs. distributed processing. Recently — cognitive psychologists seek neurological explanations; neuroscientists seek cognitive explanations. 21st century — paradigm shift using neurocognitive imagery.

**Imaging techniques:** MRI, PET, EEG. These show structures of cognition and processes involved. MRI and PET reveal brain activity during cognitive tasks. EEG measures electrical activity.

**Key terms for marks:** cognitive neuroscience; electrochemical processes; Lashley (1929); MRI, PET, EEG; paradigm shift; neurocognitive imagery.

### Flashcard 4 — Bloom's Taxonomy of Cognitive Domain

**FRONT:** Describe Bloom's Taxonomy of the Cognitive Domain. Who proposed it, when, and what are its levels? How has it been revised?

**BACK:**

**Origin:** Benjamin Bloom (1965) suggested three domains of learning: (1) Cognitive — recall, calculate, discuss, analyse, problem solve; (2) Psychomotor — dance, swim, ski, drive; (3) Affective — like, love, appreciate, fear, hate. Not mutually exclusive.

**Taxonomy (Bloom, Englehart, Furst, Hill & Krathwohl, 1956):** Six cognitive levels, each more complex:
1. **Knowledge/Knowing** — repeat back; very little elaboration.
2. **Comprehension** — rudimentary understanding; summarise/paraphrase.
3. **Application** — use concepts/principles to solve problems.
4. **Analysis** — understand relationships among parts.
5. **Synthesis** — put parts together in original manner; highest elaboration.
6. **Evaluation** — make judgments based on comparison to standard.

**Revision:** Anderson & Krathwohl (2000) propose reversing the top two — evaluation less difficult than synthesis. Hummel & Huitt (1994) confirmed first four levels form a valid hierarchy.

**Key terms for marks:** Bloom (1956/1965); three domains (cognitive, psychomotor, affective); six levels; Anderson & Krathwohl (2000) revision; Hummel & Huitt (1994); elaboration.

### Flashcard 5 — Sternberg's Information Processing Approach

**FRONT:** Explain Sternberg's information processing approach. What are the three kinds of information-processing components of intelligence? How does this approach view development?

**BACK:**

**Theorist:** Sternberg (1988). Grounded in information processing approach. Development = skills-based and continuous (not staged). Focus on intelligence. Does not differentiate between child and adult learning. No biological development facets.

**Novice-to-expert progression:** Development via feedback, self-monitoring, automatisation.

**Three components:**
1. **Metacomponents** — executive; guide planning and decision making in problem solving; identify the problem and connect with past experiences; direct actions.
2. **Performance components** — actions taken in completing a problem-solving task; weigh merit/consequences of actions vs. alternatives.
3. **Knowledge-acquisition components** — ability to learn new information; more abstract; may or may not be directly related to a current task.

This is the *componential aspect* — one part of his larger **triarchic theory of intelligence**. Another part concerns context: intelligent behaviour defined by sociocultural context — adaptation, selection, shaping (Kearsley, 2001). Links to Bruner (1977, 1986) and Vygotsky (1978).

**Key terms for marks:** Sternberg (1988); skills-based, continuous development; metacomponents, performance components, knowledge-acquisition components; triarchic theory; adaptation, selection, shaping of environment; feedback, self-monitoring, automatisation; Bruner, Vygotsky.

### Flashcard 6 — Principles of Information Processing

**FRONT:** What are the four basic principles of information processing? Cite the relevant theorists and years.

**BACK:**

Information processing = "the change (processing) of information in any manner detectable by an observer" (Huitt, 2000). Cognition = computational in nature (mind = software, brain = hardware). Neisser (1967): cognition = "how people encode, structure, store, retrieve, use or otherwise learn knowledge."

**Four principles (Huitt, 2000):**
1. **Limited capacity** — amount processable is constrained; "bottlenecks" at specific points (Broadbent, 1975; Case, 1978).
2. **Control mechanism required** — oversees encoding, transformation, processing, storage, retrieval, utilisation; executive function uses some capacity (Atkinson & Shiffrin, 1971).
3. **Two-way flow** — sensory-derived bottom-up processing combined with memory-derived top-down processing in a dynamic construction of meaning (analogous to inductive vs. deductive reasoning).
4. **Genetic/biological preparedness** — humans genetically prepared to process information in specific ways (e.g., infants preferentially look at faces; universal language milestones — cooing, babbling, telegraphic speech, overgeneralisation).

**Key terms for marks:** Neisser (1967); computational metaphor; limited capacity; bottlenecks (Broadbent, Case); executive function (Atkinson & Shiffrin, 1971); bottom-up vs. top-down processing; biological preparedness; telegraphic speech; overgeneralisation.

### Flashcard 7 — Atkinson & Shiffrin Model of Memory

**FRONT:** Describe the Atkinson & Shiffrin multi-store model of memory (1968). What are its three stages? What is the critical distinction between memory and store?

**BACK:**

**Theorists & year:** Atkinson & Shiffrin (1968). "Traditionally, the most widely used model of information processing is the stage theory model."

**Three stages:**
1. **Sensory register/memory** — holds sensory input very briefly (~½ s for vision, Sperling, 1960; ~4–5 s for hearing, Darwin et al., 1972). Portal for all information. Attention + automaticity govern what passes through.
2. **Short-term store (STS) / working memory** — holds information ~20–30 s; "active or conscious memory." Maintenance rehearsal (repetition) vs. elaborate rehearsal (linked with stored info). Generally 5 ± 2 units.
3. **Long-term store (LTS)** — rehearsed information passes here; organised into categories; resides for days, months, years, or lifetime. LTM also has types: episodic, semantic (Tulving, 1972), declarative, procedural, imagery (Paivio, 1971, 1986).

**Critical distinction:** Memory (the data being retained) ≠ Store (the structural component containing the information). Atkinson & Shiffrin postulated direct pathway from sensory register to LTS.

**Organisation of LTM:** Bruner — categorisation is fundamental. Tulving (1972) — episodic vs. semantic. Declarative (can be verbalised) vs. procedural ("how to" knowledge). Imagery (Paivio) — memory structure for pictures.

**Key terms for marks:** Atkinson & Shiffrin (1968/1969); stage theory model; sensory register, STS, LTS; Sperling (1960); Darwin et al. (1972); attention and automaticity; maintenance vs. elaborate rehearsal; 5 ± 2; memory vs. store; Bruner categorisation; Tulving (1972) episodic/semantic; Paivio (1971, 1986) imagery; Pylyshyn (2002) critique.

### Flashcard 8 — Three Storage Systems (Sensory–STM–LTM)

**FRONT:** Explain the three storage systems of memory. What are their characteristics, capacities, and durations?

**BACK:**

**Sensory Memory:** Transduction (energy conversion from environment to electrical brain signals). Retains exact copy of what is seen/heard. < ½ s (vision), ~3 s (hearing). Attention required to transfer to STM. Two ways into STM: (a) interesting feature, (b) activates known pattern.

**Short-Term Memory (STM):** Selective attention determines transfer from sensory memory. Stored as sounds (especially words) or images. Works like computer RAM. Characteristics: limited capacity (Miller, 1956 — 7 ± 2 items); brief duration (3–20 s); decay is primary mechanism of loss. Three operations within STM: iconic memory (visual images), acoustic memory (sounds, held longer), working memory (conscious, immediate use). Chunking extends capacity.

**Long-Term Memory (LTM):** Relatively permanent storage. Information stored on basis of meaning and importance. Transfer from STM involves encoding/consolidation — "not a function of time but on organising complex information in STM before encoding." Meaningfulness and emotional content aid retention. Schemas = mental models of the world; stored in interrelated networks. Top-down processing — prior knowledge affects perception (basis of bias and optical illusions). Rehearsal provides transfer into LTM.

**Key terms for marks:** transduction; exact-copy retention; selective attention; Miller (1956) 7 ± 2; iconic, acoustic, working memory; decay; chunking; encoding/consolidation; schemas; top-down processing.

### Flashcard 9 — Waugh & Norman's Primary/Secondary Memory Model

**FRONT:** Describe Waugh & Norman's (1965) primary/secondary memory model. What experimental method did they use? What did they find about interference vs. decay?

**BACK:**

**Theorists & year:** Waugh & Norman (1965). "The first modern behavioural model to travel down memory lane." Borrowed from William James's dichotomy of primary and secondary memory.

**Dualistic model:**
- **Primary Memory (PM)** — short-term storage, limited capacity, loss by displacement of old items by new ones (not pure decay). Conceptualised as a "vertical file" — if all slots filled, new item displaces old.
- **Secondary Memory (SM)** — longer-term storage. Transfer from PM requires rehearsal.

**Experimental method:** 16-digit probe task. Lists read at one digit/second or four digits/second. Purpose: determine if forgetting is decay (time-based) or interference. Logic: if decay → less recall at slower rate; if interference → no difference.

**Finding:** "The rate of forgetting for the two presentation rates is similar. Interference seems to be a greater factor than decay." Later work (Norman, 1966) confirmed.

**PM in practice:** Holds verbal information for verbatim recall (e.g., last part of sentence just heard). Recall later requires rehearsal → SM.

**Key terms for marks:** Waugh & Norman (1965); William James dichotomy; PM vs. SM; "boxes in the head"; displacement; vertical-file analogy; 16-digit probe task; interference > decay; Norman (1966); verbatim recall.

### Flashcard 10 — Levels of Processing Model (Craik & Lockhart)

**FRONT:** Explain the Levels of Processing model proposed by Craik & Lockhart (1972). How does it differ from the multi-store model? What are its criticisms?

**BACK:**

**Theorists & year:** Craik & Lockhart (1972). "A reaction against the boxes-in-the-head scheme of memory."

**Core idea:** Incoming stimuli subjected to a series of analyses — from shallow sensory analysis to deeper, more complex, abstract, and semantic analyses. "An item processed at a deep level is less likely to be forgotten than one processed at a shallow level."

**Levels:** Earliest — sensory/featural (lines, angles). Deeper — pattern recognition and meaning extraction. Deepest — long-term associations, images, stories based on past experience (Craik & Lockhart, 1972).

**"Spread" not strict sequence:** Familiar, meaningful stimuli more likely processed deeply. The fact that we can perceive at meaningful levels before analysing at primitive levels "casts grave doubts on the original levels-of-processing formulation." Possibly "simply different types of processing."

**Rehearsal distinction:** In multi-store models, rehearsal transfers info to longer store. In LOP, rehearsal maintains info at one level (maintenance rehearsal — not better retention) or elaborates to deeper level (elaborate rehearsal — better retention).

**Three criticisms (Baddeley, 1978):** (1) "seems to say little more than that meaningful events are well remembered" — mundane conclusion; (2) "vague and generally untestable"; (3) "circular — any events well remembered are designated deeply processed, with no objective index of depth."

**Supporting experiments:** Craik & Tulving (1975): structural < phonological < semantic decision for recognition. Zinchenko (1962): Level of Recall (LOR) determined by goal of action. Rogers, Kuiper & Kirker (1977): self-reference effect.

**Key terms for marks:** Craik & Lockhart (1972); LOP; shallow vs. deep processing; "boxes in the head" reaction; maintenance vs. elaborate rehearsal; three criticisms (Baddeley, 1978); Craik & Tulving (1975); proofreading vs. reading for gist; Zinchenko LOR; self-reference effect.

### Flashcard 11 — Memory and Brain / Cellular Bases

**FRONT:** What are the cellular bases of learning and memory? Describe Hebb's Law, Long-Term Potentiation, and the NMDA receptor mechanism. How have case studies (H.M., R.B.) informed our understanding of memory's neural substrate?

**BACK:**

**Squire (1987):** Learning = acquiring new information; memory = persistence of learning. Three stages: encoding (acquisition + consolidation), storage, retrieval.

**Case H.M. (1953):** Bilateral medial temporal lobe removal by Scoville (neurosurgeon) and Milner (psychologist). Severe anterograde amnesia — cannot form new long-term memories. Normal STM. Intelligence above normal, no perceptual/language problems. Bilateral hippocampal damage → selective memory deficit.

**Case R.B.:** Ischemic episode during bypass surgery. Dense anterograde amnesia. Lesions restricted to hippocampus, specifically CA1 pyramidal cells. Supports hippocampus crucial for forming new long-term memories.

**Medial temporal lobe structures:** amygdala, hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, parahippocampal and perirhinal cortical areas (Brodmann's areas 35, 36). Amygdala = emotional memory (Zola et al., 1993) — NOT part of LTM acquisition system.

**Hebb's Law (Donald Hebb, 1949):** "If a synapse between two neurons is repeatedly activated at about the same time the postsynaptic neuron fires, the structure or the chemistry of the neuron changes and the synapse will be strengthened" — Hebbian learning.

**Long-Term Potentiation (LTP):** Neural circuits in hippocampus subjected to repeated, intense electrical stimulation develop cells more sensitive to stimuli. Enhanced response can last weeks or longer (Baddeley, 1993). Possible mechanism for long-term learning/retention.

**NMDA receptor:** Excitatory input + postsynaptic depolarisation needed for LTP. Doubly gated N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor on dendritic spines. Glutamate = major excitatory transmitter. AP5 blocks NMDA receptors → prevents LTP induction but not maintenance. NMDA receptors central to *producing* LTP; maintenance depends on non-NMDA receptors.

**Imaging:** Haxby & Ungerleider (1996) — HERA model (Hemispheric Encoding/Retrieval Asymmetry). Encoding = left hemisphere; retrieval = right hemisphere; dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. Schacter et al. (1996) — implicit and explicit retrieval subserved by separate brain systems; bilateral occipital cortex (area 19) deactivation during priming.

**Key terms for marks:** Squire (1987); H.M. (1953); Scoville & Milner; R.B. (CA1 pyramidal cells); amygdala vs. hippocampus; Hebb (1949) Hebbian learning; LTP; NMDA receptor; glutamate; AP5; HERA model (Haxby/Ungerleider, 1996); Schacter et al. (1996) priming.

### Flashcard 12 — Memory Consolidation

**FRONT:** What is memory consolidation? What role does the hippocampus play? How does the evidence from amnesia support the consolidation theory?

**BACK:**

**Definition:** "Memories are solidified in long-term stores over days, weeks, months, and years" (Squire, 1987). Cognitive-neuroscience view: "biological changes that underlie the long-term retention of learned information."

**Hippocampus's role — not storage:** Because medial temporal lobe damage does NOT wipe out most declarative memories formed over a lifetime, "the hippocampus is not the repository of stored knowledge. Rather, the medial temporal lobe appears to support the process of forming new memories; that is, the hippocampal region is critical for the consolidation of information in long-term memory."

**Evidence:** Amnesics have retrograde amnesia for memories from one to a few years prior to damage — pattern that supports a role in consolidation, not storage.

**Neural mechanism:** Consolidation "strengthens the associations between multiple stimulus inputs and activations of previously stored information." Hippocampus coordinates this strengthening, but effects take place in neocortex. Once consolidation complete, hippocampus no longer required for storage or retrieval.

**Two steps of encoding:** Acquisition (registers inputs in sensory buffers) + Consolidation (creates stronger representation over time). Storage = result of acquisition + consolidation.

**Diencephalic amnesia:** Midline diencephalon damage also causes amnesia. Key structures: dorsomedial nucleus of thalamus + mammillary bodies. Alcoholic Korsakoff's Syndrome: chronic alcoholism → vitamin deficiency → brain damage. Sergei Korsakoff (late 19th century) reported anterograde and retrograde amnesia.

**Key terms for marks:** consolidation (biological changes underlying long-term retention); hippocampus coordinates consolidation but NOT storage repository; effects in neocortex; retrograde amnesia temporally limited; acquisition vs. consolidation; dorsomedial nucleus of thalamus; mammillary bodies; Korsakoff's syndrome.

### Flashcard 13 — Alfred Binet & IQ

**FRONT:** Describe Alfred Binet's contribution to the measurement of intelligence. What was the Binet-Simon Scale? How was the IQ concept derived?

**BACK:**

**Alfred Binet (1857–1911):** Commissioned by French government to identify mentally deficient children.

**Binet-Simon Scale (1905):** 30 problems (pass/fail) measuring ability to understand and reason with objects. Tried on 50 children. Innovation: first measured intelligence during a clinical interview (not laboratories/physical measurements). Abilities measured: memory, imagery, imagination, attention, comprehension, aesthetic sentiment, moral sentiment, muscular strength, motor ability, hand-eye coordination.

**1908 revision:** Items arranged by age levels. "The highest age level a child could perform successfully was his mental age."

**1911 revision:** Age range extended through adulthood. Five items per age level. Abilities: language, auditory processing, visual processing, learning/memory, problem solving.

**IQ concept:** William Stern (1912/1914) — IQ = (Mental Age / Chronological Age) × 100. Lewis Terman (1916) — Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scale.

**Binet's definition (1890):** "intelligence, that is to say, reasoning, judgment, memory, and the power of abstraction." Five components: abstract reasoning, comprehension, clear direction of thought, purposeful thinking, self-corrective judgment.

**Key terms for marks:** Binet-Simon scale (1905); Theodore Simon; 30 items, 50 children; mental age; IQ = (MA/CA) × 100; William Stern (1912/1914); Stanford-Binet (1916); Terman; clinical interview; age-graded norms; five components.

### Flashcard 14 — Spearman's Two-Factor Theory

**FRONT:** Explain Spearman's two-factor theory of intelligence. What are the "g" and "s" factors? Who were his critics?

**BACK:**

**Charles Spearman (1904):** "No single event in the history of mental testing has proved to be of such momentous importance as Spearman's proposal of his famous two-factor theory" — Guilford (1954).

**Core theory:** Intercorrelations between intelligence tests are due to a general intellective factor "g" common to all, plus specific factors "s" specific to particular tests.

**"g" factor:** General factor / "mental energy." Important in every mental act. Tests with high "g" loadings: abstract reasoning, comprehension, problem solving. Confirmatory support: Jensen (1998), Carroll (1993, Stratum III).

**Method:** Factor analysis; tetrad difference (F = r₁₃r₂₄ − r₁₄r₂₃ ≈ 0).

**Group factors:** Verbal ability, numerical ability, mental speed, mechanical ability, attention, imagination (Guilford, 1953).

**Five quantitative principles (Spearman, 1923):** conative control, fatigue, mental energy, primordial potencies, retentivity.

**Critics:**
- **Thorndike (1926):** Intercorrelations too small; common element in tasks, not the individual. Three intelligences: social, concrete, abstract.
- **Thomson (1939):** Sampling theory — "g" not a basic entity but a constant combination of ability elements.
- **Thurstone (1935):** Seven primary mental abilities (later found hierarchical with "g" at top).

**Key terms for marks:** Spearman (1904); g factor ("mental energy"); s factor; "The abilities of man" (1927); factor analysis; tetrad difference; group factors; Thorndike (1926) three intelligences; Thomson (1939) sampling theory; Thurstone (1935) seven primary abilities; Stratum III (Carroll).

### Flashcard 15 — Guilford's Structure of Intellect (SOI)

**FRONT:** What is Guilford's Structure of Intellect model? Describe its three dimensions and the original vs. modified versions.

**BACK:**

**J. P. Guilford (1956 first version; modified 1977):** Intelligence is not monolithic but a combination of multiple, relatively independent abilities. Rejected Spearman's "g."

**Three dimensions (cube representation):**
1. **Contents (5):** Visual (retinal image), Auditory (cochlear image), Symbolic (signs with no intrinsic meaning, e.g., numerals), Semantic (words/sentences), Behavioural (acts of individuals).
2. **Operations (5):** Cognition (understand/comprehend/discover), Memory (memorise), Divergent Production (generating multiple solutions), Convergent Production (deducing single solution), Evaluation (judging accuracy/consistency/validity).
3. **Products (6):** Unit (single item), Class (set sharing attributes), Relation (connection — opposites, associations, sequences, analogies), System (organisation/network of interacting parts), Transformation (changes/conversions/mutations), Implication (predictions/inferences/consequences).

**Original (1956):** 4 contents × 5 operations × 6 products = **120 factors**. Over 100 empirically verified.

**Modified (1977):** Figural content split into Visual + Auditory → 5 × 5 × 6 = **150 cells**.

**Open system:** Allows newly discovered categories to be added in any direction.

**Criticisms (Eysenck, 1972):** (1) concentrated on restricted-range populations → reduced "g"; (2) used orthogonal rotation → ignored inter-factor correlations; (3) factors too narrow for practical prediction.

**Key terms for marks:** SOI (1956); 1977 modification; 150 cells (originally 120); cube representation; three dimensions (Contents/Operations/Products); five contents, five operations, six products; rejection of "g"; Eysenck's criticisms.

### Flashcard 16 — Gardner's Theory of Multiple Intelligences (including Bodily-Kinesthetic)

**FRONT:** Explain Howard Gardner's Theory of Multiple Intelligences. List and briefly describe each intelligence. What are the eight criteria for a "candidate intelligence"? Define bodily-kinesthetic intelligence.

**BACK:**

**Howard Gardner (1983, *Frames of Mind*):** Intelligence = "a bio-psychological potential to process information that can be activated in a cultural setting to solve problems or create products that are of value in a culture."

**Eight criteria:** (1) potential for brain isolation by brain damage, (2) evolutionary history, (3) core operations, (4) susceptibility to encoding, (5) developmental progression, (6) idiot-savants/prodigies/exceptional people, (7) experimental psychology support, (8) psychometric support.

**Eight intelligences (naturalist added 1999; existential considered 9th):**
1. **Linguistic** — facility with words; writers, poets, lawyers, speakers.
2. **Logical-Mathematical** — detect patterns, reason deductively; scientists, mathematicians, engineers.
3. **Musical** — performance, composition, appreciation of musical patterns; sensitivity to rhythm, pitch, meter, timbre.
4. **Bodily-Kinesthetic** — potential to use whole body or parts of body to solve problems; ability to use mental abilities to coordinate bodily movements. Learn by muscular movement/doing. Careers: athletes, dancers, actors, surgeons, builders, police, soldiers.
5. **Spatial** — recognising and manipulating patterns in wide or confined spaces; pilots, sculptors, architects, chess players.
6. **Interpersonal** — understand intentions, motivations, desires of others; extrovert, sensitive to moods/feelings.
7. **Intrapersonal** — understand oneself; introspective, self-reflective; philosophers, psychologists.
8. **Naturalistic** — relating to natural surroundings; recognition/classification of species (added 1999).
9. **Existential** — contemplate phenomena beyond sensory data (considered 9th).

**Criticisms:** Morgan (1996) — cognitive styles, not constructs; Hunt (2001) — not measurable; Eysenck (1990) — different abilities, not intelligences; Sternberg & Frensch (1990) — strange to describe tone-deaf as "unintelligent"; not empirical; incompatible with "g."

**Key terms for marks:** Gardner (1983); eight criteria; eight/nine intelligences; bio-psychological potential; bodily-kinesthetic = use whole body to solve problems; personal intelligences; Project Zero; criticisms (Morgan, Hunt, Eysenck, Sternberg & Frensch).

### Flashcard 17 — Sternberg's Triarchic Theory of Intelligence

**FRONT:** What is Sternberg's Triarchic Theory of Intelligence? Describe each of the three subtheories. What criticisms has it faced?

**BACK:**

**Sternberg (1985, *Beyond IQ*):** "A comprehensive theory, because it takes into account social and contextual factors apart from human abilities" (Li, 1996). Previous theories "not incorrect, but rather, incomplete."

**Three subtheories:**
1. **Componential (Analytical)** — relates to internal world. Three component types: metacomponents (executive — planning, monitoring), performance components (executing strategies), knowledge-acquisition components (gaining/storing new knowledge). Corresponds to academic problem-solving.
2. **Experiential (Creative)** — relates internal world to external reality. Two parts: novelty (never experienced before; adept people find new ways) and automation (process performed multiple times, now done with little thought). "Being skilled in one component does not ensure that you are skilled in the other also" (Sternberg, 1997).
3. **Contextual (Practical)** — relates to external world. Three processes: adaptation (change within oneself), shaping (change the environment), selection (find new alternate environment). Often called "street smarts."

**Central thesis:** Intelligent behaviour arises from balance between analytical, creative, and practical abilities. Central feature = adaptability (Cianciolo & Sternberg, 2004).

**Criticisms:**
- **Gottfredson (2003):** Unempirical; "practical intelligence" = task-specific knowledge, not broad cognition.
- **Das (2004):** Difficult to translate into psychometric instruments.
- Creativity may simply be the product of high intelligence (separate factor debate).

**Key terms for marks:** Triarchic theory (1985); three subtheories (Componential/Experiential/Contextual = Analytical/Creative/Practical); metacomponents/performance/knowledge-acquisition; novelty & automation; adaptation/shaping/selection; "street smarts"; successful intelligence; Gottfredson (2003) and Das (2004) criticisms.

### Flashcard 18 — PASS Theory of Intelligence

**FRONT:** What is the PASS theory of intelligence? Describe its four components and how they relate to Luria's three functional units.

**BACK:**

**Theorists & year:** Das, Nagliery & Kirby (1994). Grounded in Luria's neuropsychology (1966, 1973, 1980). Theory-based, multidimensional; accounts for how input is received, processed, and reorganised before response.

**Four components (PASS):**
1. **Planning** — programming, regulation, verification of behaviour; asking questions, problem solving, self-monitoring. Located in frontal lobes, especially prefrontal cortex.
2. **Attention-Arousal** — keeping the person alert (brain stem + reticular activating system); frontal lobes + lower cortex. Only under optimal arousal can complex attention occur.
3. **Simultaneous processing** — occipital-parietal areas; essential aspect = surveyability (each element related to every other). E.g., "draw a triangle above a square that is to the left of a circle under a cross."
4. **Successive processing** — fronto-temporal areas; serial order, each component related to next. E.g., decoding syntax, articulating speech.

**Luria's three functional units:**
- First unit: cortical arousal/attention (brain stem, reticular activating system)
- Second unit: simultaneous/successive processing (occipital-parietal / fronto-temporal)
- Third unit: programming, regulation, executive functioning (prefrontal lobes)

**Knowledge base:** Integral component; all processes embedded within it; represents cultural and social background knowledge.

**Cognitive Assessment System (CAS):** Four subscales named after PASS. Used for diagnosis, eligibility, discrepancy determination, instructional planning.

**Key terms for marks:** PASS (Das, Nagliery & Kirby, 1994); Luria's three functional units (1966, 1973, 1980); brain stem / reticular activating system; occipital-parietal (simultaneous) vs. fronto-temporal (successive); prefrontal cortex (planning); surveyability; serial order; knowledge base; CAS.

### Flashcard 19 — Creativity: Meaning, Aspects, Stages

**FRONT:** Define creativity. What are its aspects (components) as described by Guilford and Torrance? What are the four stages of creative discovery (Wallas, 1926)?

**BACK:**

**Definition:** "Appropriate novelty" (Hennessey & Amabile, 1988). "Goal-directed thinking which is unusual, novel and useful." Newell, Shaw & Simon (1963) — four criteria: (a) novelty and usefulness, (b) rejects previously accepted ideas, (c) requires intense motivation and persistence, (d) results from organising unclear situation in new way. Smith, Ward & Finke (1995) — four process characteristics: imaginative, purposeful, original, has value. Beghetto & Kaufman (2007) — little-c (everyday) vs. Big-C (eminent) creativity.

**Aspects — Guilford (1986):** Creative thinking involves divergent thinking. Components: fluency, flexibility, originality, elaboration, sensitivity to problems, redefinition abilities, freedom from functional fixedness.

**Aspects — Torrance (1966, 1974) TTCT:** (1) Fluency (number of relevant ideas), (2) Flexibility (variety of responses), (3) Originality (statistically infrequent ideas), (4) Elaboration (added ideas), (5) Abstractness of Titles (degree beyond concrete labelling), (6) Resistance to Premature Closure (psychological openness).

**Four stages — Wallas (1926):**
1. **Preparation** — gather information, attempt solutions, trial-and-error.
2. **Incubation** — solution exists but not clear; sink into unconscious; allowed to rest.
3. **Illumination (Insight)** — sudden emergence of new solution/idea/relationship.
4. **Verification** — test, try, check the solution; modification may occur.

**Two key characteristics of creative people:** (1) Clear sense of purpose, (2) Store of knowledge and techniques.

**Key terms for marks:** appropriate novelty (Hennessey & Amabile, 1988); Newell, Shaw & Simon (1963) four criteria; Smith, Ward & Finke (1995) four process characteristics; little-c vs. Big-C (Beghetto & Kaufman, 2007); Guilford (1986) divergent thinking; fluency, flexibility, originality, elaboration; Torrance (1966, 1974) six components; Wallas (1926) four stages.

### Flashcard 20 — Investment & Confluence Theory of Creativity

**FRONT:** Describe Sternberg's Investment and Confluence Theory of Creativity (2006). What are the six resources? How do they combine?

**BACK:**

**Sternberg (2006):** Creativity requires a confluence of six distinct but interrelated resources.

**Six resources:**
1. **Intellectual abilities:** (a) Synthetic skill — see problems in new ways, escape conventional thinking; (b) Analytic skill — recognise which ideas are worth pursuing; (c) Practical-contextual skill — persuade others of value of ideas. All three essential; analytic alone = critical, not creative; synthetic alone = unscrutinised ideas.
2. **Knowledge:** Must know enough to move field forward. But knowledge can produce closed/entrenched perspective.
3. **Thinking styles:** Legislative style (preference for thinking in new ways) particularly important. Also global vs. local thinking.
4. **Personality:** Willingness to overcome obstacles, take sensible risks, tolerate ambiguity, self-efficacy. Creative people "buy low and sell high" — defy the crowd, seek opposition.
5. **Motivation:** Intrinsic, task-focused motivation essential (Amabile, 1983). People rarely do creative work unless they love what they do.
6. **Environment:** Supportive and rewarding of creative ideas.

**Confluence principle:** More than simple sum of levels. Three mechanisms: (1) thresholds — below minimum on some component, creativity impossible; (2) partial compensation — strength on one compensates weakness on another; (3) interactions — high levels on two components multiplicatively enhance creativity.

**Key terms for marks:** Sternberg (2006); investment theory; six resources; synthetic/analytic/practical-contextual skills; legislative style; "buy low and sell high"; Amabile (1983) intrinsic motivation; thresholds; partial compensation; multiplicative interaction; self-efficacy; tolerance of ambiguity.

### Flashcard 21 — Creativity and Intelligence (Relationship)

**FRONT:** What is the relationship between creativity and intelligence? Describe the threshold hypothesis, the triangularity hypothesis, and the certification hypothesis.

**BACK:**

**Weak relationship:** Most studies show weak correlation between psychometric intelligence and creativity. Torrance (1977): median r = .20 (178 correlations between IQ and TTCT). Factor analyses yielded separate factors.

**Extreme independence position:** Creativity viewed as accessible to everyone, dependent on domain-specific knowledge and deliberate practice, not influenced by intelligence.

**Terman (1925):** 1,528 highly gifted children (mean IQ 151) — most achieved occupational success, but none showed noteworthy creativity. High IQ insufficient for creativity.

**Threshold hypothesis (Guilford, 1967):** IQ ≥ 120 (approximately one standard deviation above mean) necessary but not sufficient for creativity. Scatter plots show triangular pattern — no data points in low-IQ/high-creativity quadrant. Widely accepted but empirical tests scarce.

**Triangularity hypothesis:** Refers to the triangular pattern in scatter plots of IQ vs. creativity.

**Certification hypothesis (Hayes, 1989):** Most opportunities to display recognisable creativity (architecture, science) require high formal education. Since academic performance correlates with IQ, society denies low-IQ individuals the chance to express creativity. Doubts intrinsic links between creativity and intelligence.

**Key terms for marks:** Terman (1925) 1,528 gifted children; Torrance (1977) median r = .20; separate factors; Guilford (1967) threshold hypothesis (IQ ≥ 120); triangularity hypothesis; Hayes (1989) certification hypothesis; domain-specific knowledge; deliberate practice; little-c vs. Big-C.

### Flashcard 22 — Problem Solving: Approaches & Strategies

**FRONT:** What are the major approaches to problem solving? Describe each approach and the master strategies (algorithm vs. heuristic).

**BACK:**

**Definition:** "A higher-order cognitive process requiring modulation and control of more routine skills." Baron (2001): "efforts to develop or choose among various responses in order to attain desired goals." Two processes: problem orientation (motivational/attitudinal/affective) + problem-solving skills (cognitive-behavioural steps).

**Four approaches:**
1. **Traditional/Associative learning:** S-R associations; problem difficulty = strength of correct vs. incorrect associations. Extinction weakens wrong, reinforcement strengthens correct. Stresses transfer of prior learning.
2. **Gestalt:** Emphasises structure of problem situation and formation of new combinations. Rearrangement of objects. Insight = sudden discovery. (See Flashcard 24.)
3. **Information Processing & Computer Simulation:** Problem solving requires registering info from environment + operating on/modifying/transforming it + retrieving factual and procedural knowledge from LTM. Not a single cognitive process but many organised activities.
4. **Problem Solving as Modelling:** Construction of situation-specific model (case model). Domain model + task model → competency theory.

**Master strategies:**
- **Algorithm:** Specific procedure that guarantees a solution if followed correctly (e.g., multiplication rules). Includes systematic random search (orderly, keeps record) and unsystematic random search (no record, may repeat errors).
- **Heuristic:** Rules of thumb; do not guarantee success but permit faster solutions (e.g., familiar letter combinations in anagrams). Psychologists have paid more attention to heuristics than algorithms.

**Key terms for marks:** problem orientation vs. problem-solving skills; algorithm; heuristic; systematic vs. unsystematic random search; transfer of prior learning; GPS (Newell, Shaw & Simon, 1958); problem space; operators; competency theory; domain model; task model.

### Flashcard 23 — Gestalt Approach to Problem Solving

**FRONT:** Explain the Gestalt approach to problem solving. What are reproductive vs. productive thinking? What role does insight play?

**BACK:**

**Core ideas:** Gestalt psychologists emphasised the structure of the problem situation and formation of new combinations of old ideas. Interested in how people solve problems by rearrangement of objects.

**Kohler (1925, *The Mentality of Apes*):** Fruit hung from cage top; sticks and crates available. Solution required stacking boxes/joining sticks = reorganisation of objects into new structure. Correct organisation occurred as a flash of insight.

**Insight:** "The sudden discovery of the correct solution following a period of incorrect attempts based primarily on trial and error. Insightful solutions seem to occur in a flash."

**Reproductive vs. productive thinking (Wertheimer, 1959):**
- Reproductive thinking = applying tried-and-true paths; reproducing steps known to yield a workable answer via rote memory.
- Productive thinking = requires insight and creativity; see new way of organising problem/structuring elements of thought and perception.

**Nine-dot problem:** Connect nine dots with four straight lines without lifting pencil. Requires restructuring — lines must extend beyond the visual boundaries.

**Sagacity:** "Ability to see into the situation and discriminate important aspects." Differs from learning — involves sensitivity to detail and discernment of what is important.

**Insight vs. non-insight problems:** Insight = sudden; non-insight = gradual "feeling of warmth." Metacognitive assessments accurate for non-insight, not for insight. Gestalt psychologists: "people are generally not efficient at realising that a new problem can be solved with information already at their disposal."

**Key terms for marks:** Gestalt; Kohler (1925) *The Mentality of Apes*; insight (sudden illumination); reproductive vs. productive thinking (Wertheimer, 1959); reorganisation/restructuring; nine-dot problem; sagacity; Einstellung (mental set); functional fixedness; feeling of warmth; metacognition.

### Flashcard 24 — Well-Defined vs. Ill-Defined Problems / Types of Problems

**FRONT:** Distinguish between well-defined and ill-defined problems. What are Greeno's (1978) three categories of well-defined problems? List the characteristics of difficult problems.

**BACK:**

**Well-defined problem:** Nature of problem and information needed are available and clear; straightforward judgments about appropriateness of potential solutions. E.g., mathematical equation, jigsaw puzzle.

**Ill-defined problem:** Specific nature of problem may be unclear; information required less obvious. E.g., "how to bring peace."

**Greeno (1978) — three categories of well-defined problems:**
1. **Arrangement problems:** Solver must rearrange/recombine elements to satisfy criterion. Several arrangements possible but only one/few solve it. E.g., anagram problems, jigsaw puzzles.
2. **Inducing structure problems:** Identify existing relationships among elements, then construct a new relationship. E.g., number series completion.
3. **Transformation problems:** Change initial state to goal state. E.g., Tower of Hanoi. Primarily requires planning skills based on means-end analysis.

**Characteristics of difficult problems (16 named impediments):** Intransparency, commencement opacity, continuation opacity, polytely (multiple goals), inexpressiveness, opposition, transience, complexity, enumerability, connectivity, heterogeneity, dynamics, temporal constraints, temporal sensitivity, phase effects, dynamic unpredictability.

**Key terms for marks:** well-defined vs. ill-defined problems; Greeno (1978); arrangement problems; inducing-structure problems; transformation problems; Tower of Hanoi; anagram; jigsaw; means-end analysis; intransparency; commencement opacity; continuation opacity; polytely; phase effects; dynamic unpredictability.

### Flashcard 25 — Techniques of Problem Solving

**FRONT:** What are the specific techniques of problem solving? Describe each of the major techniques. What are Wickelgren's seven general problem-solving strategies?

**BACK:**

**Specific techniques (Unit 2):**
1. **Generate and Test:** Generate possible solutions, test against criteria. Loses effectiveness with many possibilities.
2. **Means-Ends Analysis:** Divide into sub-problems; detect difference between current state and goal state and reduce it. Involves subgoals.
3. **Backward Search:** Start at goal state, work backward. Useful when goal uniquely specified. Wickelgren (1974): "Working backward is only viable when the goal state is uniquely well defined."
4. **Planning Strategy / Analogy:** Split into simple and complex aspects; solve simple first. Analogy = using solution to earlier/similar problem.
5. **Thinking Aloud (Ericsson & Simon):** Concurrent verbalisation (verbalise info as attended to) vs. retrospective verbalisation (about earlier processes). Protocol = verbal description. Newell (1977): divide protocol into phrases, construct problem behaviour graph. Schooler, Ohlsson & Brooks (1993): verbalisation interferes with insight problems — non-reportable/unconscious processes.
6. **Other strategies:** Abstraction, Divide & Conquer, Hypothesis Testing, Lateral Thinking, Method of Focal Objects, Reduction, Research, Root Cause Analysis, Trial-and-Error, Brainstorming.

**Wickelgren's seven strategies (state-action tree):**
(i) Inference, (ii) Classification of action sequences (equivalence classes), (iii) State evaluation and hill climbing, (iv) Subgoals, (v) Contradiction, (vi) Working backward, (vii) Finding relations between problems.

**Key terms for marks:** generate and test; means-ends analysis; subgoal; backward search (Wickelgren, 1974); planning strategy; analogy; concurrent vs. retrospective verbalisation (Ericsson & Simon); protocol; problem behaviour graph (Newell, 1977); Schooler, Ohlsson & Brooks (1993); state-action tree; equivalence classes; hill climbing; brainstorming; IDEAL (Bransford & Stein: Identify, Define, Explore, Act, Look back).

### Flashcard 26 — Functional Fixedness

**FRONT:** What is functional fixedness? Who discovered it? Describe the candle experiment and Simmel's coin problem.

**BACK:**

**Definition:** Functional fixedness, discovered by Duncker (1935), refers to the tendency to see objects as having only a single, typical use. "The prototypical function dominates the way we think."

**Duncker's candle experiment:** Participant in room with table holding three cardboard boxes filled with candles, tacks and matches, plus other objects. Task: mount candles at eye level on wall. Only 43% solved it. Hypothesis: participants fixated on the common function of boxes (containers). When repeated with contents emptied onto table (boxes empty), all participants solved it — by mounting empty boxes on wall as platforms. Emptying the boxes broke functional fixedness.

**Simmel's coin problem (1953):** Eight coins, one counterfeit (lighter); find using balance only twice. Most people initially divide 4 vs. 4, then 2 vs. 2 — cannot identify within two weighings. Correct: divide into groups of 3, 3, and 2. "When the obvious way of using things keeps us from seeing the correct way, then we are functionally fixed."

**Relation to other barriers:** Functional fixedness = cognitive barrier; lack of transfer skills; perception that a particular object/concept has only one use. Pair with superficial transference (linking words/variables between problems instead of deeper structures).

**Remedy:** Overcome by reorganisation of the problem; realise obvious ways do not work first.

**Key terms for marks:** Duncker (1935); functional fixedness; prototypical function; candle problem (43% vs. 100%); Simmel (1953) coin problem; cognitive barriers; lack of transfer; superficial transference; reorganisation.

### Flashcard 27 — Environmental & Cultural Blocks in Problem Solving

**FRONT:** What are environmental and cultural blocks in problem solving? List and describe the named sub-types of each.

**BACK:**

**Five barrier types overall:** Perceptual, Emotional, Intellectual, Expressive, Environmental, Cultural (p. 51).

**Environmental blocks (8):** "Caused by external obstacles in the social or physical environment."
1. **Management style** — how we are managed influences attitude and freedom to create.
2. **Distractions** — excessive noise and interruptions.
3. **Physical discomfort** — e.g., poorly designed chairs causing irritability.
4. **Lack of support** — lack of specialist information, advice, skills, resources, authority, or encouragement.
5. **Stress** — pressure of work and deadlines; powerful block, hindering creative thinking.
6. **Lack of communication** — inability to get required information.
7. **Monotonous work** — dulls enthusiasm; puts us on "automatic pilot."
8. **Expectations of others** — peers/superiors satisfied with regular solutions discourage innovation.

**Cultural blocks (6):** "Result from conditioning to accept what is expected or 'normal' in a given situation."
1. **Unquestioning acceptance of status quo** — conforming to established ideas/methods.
2. **Dislike of change** — tradition preferred; any change feels threatening.
3. **Fantasy and humour not productive** — belief they have no place in serious problem solving; but "fantasy and humour are connected by one common feature — the unlikely combination of ideas."
4. **Feelings, intuition, subjective judgments unreliable** — strong bias toward reason and logic; but "even in mathematics, intuition is often reported as playing a key role."
5. **Over-emphasis on competition or cooperation** — competitive → unwilling to listen; cooperative → avoid standing out.
6. **Taboos** — some actions/ideas excluded as distasteful or contravening moral codes.

**Overcoming:** Re-learning for own blocks; learning to sidestep for others' blocks.

### Flashcard 28 — Language Acquisition: Stages & Building Blocks

**FRONT:** What are the building blocks of language and the stages of language acquisition? Compare the four major theories of language acquisition.

**BACK:**

**Building blocks:**
- **Phonemes** — smallest distinguishable units in a language.
- **Morphemes** — smallest meaningful units in a language (e.g., "disliked" = dis + lik + ed).
- **Syntax** — rules governing how words can be meaningfully arranged into phrases and sentences.
- **Semantics** — meaning of words and sentences.
- **Pragmatics** — use of words in context.

**Stages:**
1. **Cooing** — vowel sounds; indistinguishable across languages, including deaf infants.
2. **Babbling** — consonant + vowel sounds (Oller & Eilers, 1998); deaf infants stop; hearing infants shift to phonemes of own language (Locke, 1994).
3. **Holophrases (one-word utterances)** — limited vowels/consonants; usually nouns; overextension errors. By 18 months, vocabulary 3–100 words.
4. **Telegraphic speech (two-word utterances)** — 1.5–2.5 years; articles/prepositions omitted. E.g., "want juice," "doggie bite."
5. **Basic adult sentence structure** — by about age 4. Vocabulary ~300 at 2, ~1000 at 3. By age 5, complex constructions. By age 10, fundamentally adult language.

**Four theories:**
- **Behaviourist (Skinner, 1957):** Imitation + reinforcement. "No essential difference between how a rat learns a maze and a child learns to speak."
- **Innateness (Chomsky, 1957/1959):** LAD; innate language faculty. Children learn grammar without explicit instruction.
- **Cognitive (Piaget):** Language is part of overall intellectual development. Must understand concept before acquiring language form.
- **Interactionist (Bruner, 1966, 1968):** CDS (child-directed speech), scaffolding, LASS.

**Key terms for marks:** phoneme, morpheme, syntax, semantics, pragmatics; cooing, babbling, holophrase, overextension error, telegraphic speech; Skinner (1957), Chomsky, Piaget, Bruner (LASS, scaffolding, CDS); Genie case; Williams Syndrome.

### Flashcard 29 — Chomsky's Innateness Theory of Language

**FRONT:** Explain Chomsky's innateness theory of language. What is the LAD? What evidence supports innateness? What are its limitations?

**BACK:**

**Chomsky (1957, 1959):** Children born with innate capacity for learning language. "Humans are destined to speak." Children discover grammar based on inborn grammar. LAD = Language Acquisition Device — innate faculty for language. Chomsky did not suggest an English child is born knowing English; "all human languages share common principles. It is the child's task to establish how the specific language s/he hears expresses these underlying principles."

**Principles and Parameters / Universal Grammar:** Principles = general features common to all languages; parameters = variables accounting for diversity (e.g., SOV vs. SVO sentence patterns).

**Slobin's modification:** LAD is "not a set of linguistic categories but some sort of process mechanism — a set of procedures and inference rules."

**Evidence supporting innateness:**
1. Human vocal tract peculiarly adapted to speech (Slobin).
2. Broca's and Wernicke's areas — specific brain areas with linguistic functions.
3. Chimpanzee experiments — apes learn individual "words" but no grammatical competence.
4. Creole formation (Bickerton): slaves produced restricted pidgin; children turned it into full creole.
5. Nicaraguan signing creole (Pinker, 1994): deaf children created complex sign language.

**Limitations:**
- Chomsky's work was theoretical (grammar); "He did not study real children."
- Takes no account of interaction between children and caretakers.
- Ignores functions of language / why child wants to speak.
- Jim case (Bard & Sachs, 1977): hearing child of deaf parents, exposed to TV/radio, limited progress until speech therapist worked with him — "Simply being exposed to language was not enough."

**Key terms for marks:** LAD; Universal Grammar; Principles and Parameters; critical period; species-specific module; Chomsky (1957, 1959); creolisation/pidgin→creole (Bickerton); Nicaraguan signing creole; Broca's & Wernicke's areas; Slobin's process-mechanism reformulation; Jim case (Bard & Sachs, 1977).

### Flashcard 30 — Single-System vs. Dual-System Hypotheses of Language

**FRONT:** What are the single-system and dual-system hypotheses regarding multilingual brain representation? What does the evidence suggest?

**BACK:**

**Single-system hypothesis:** Two or more languages are represented in just one system or brain region. Supported by Hernandez et al. (2001) in early multilinguals.

**Dual-system hypothesis:** Different languages are represented in separate systems of mind (De Houwer, 1995; Paradis, 1981). E.g., German information stored in a physically different part of brain than English information.

**Evidence — brain-damaged aphasics:** If dual-system → different degrees of impairment across languages; if single-system → roughly equal impairment. Results equivocal: sometimes first language recovers first, sometimes second language, sometimes equal.

**Meinzer et al. (2006):** Early bilingual aphasic trained in native language only → significant recovery of L1, no change in L2. "Results suggest at least some duality of structure."

**Cortical stimulation (Ojemann & Whitaker, 1978):** Two bilingual epilepsy patients; mild electrical stimulation. "Results suggest some aspects of the two languages may be represented singly. Other aspects may be represented separately."

**Related memory-store models:**
- Kroll & Sholl (1992): Concept Mediation account — lexical in language-specific stores, semantic in common conceptual store.
- Paivio (1986): Both in language-specific stores.

**Key terms for marks:** single-system hypothesis; dual-system hypothesis; Hernandez et al. (2001); De Houwer (1995); Paradis (1981); Meinzer et al. (2006); Ojemann & Whitaker (1978); cortical stimulation; bilingual aphasia; Concept Mediation account (Kroll & Sholl, 1992); Paivio (1986); early vs. late bilinguals.

### Flashcard 31 — Clark & Clark: Three Elements of Language Expression

**FRONT:** What are Clark & Clark's (1977) three elements of language expression and human communication? Describe each. What are the five stages of speech production?

**BACK:**

**Three elements (Clark & Clark, 1977):**

1. **Speech Acts:** Speakers intend to influence listeners; listeners must recognise intentions. All utterances classifiable by type (assertions, verbal commitments, thanks, warnings, commands). Searle (1969): some speech acts are indirect (e.g., "Do you live in a barn?" = request to close door). Meaning depends on context (Gibbs, 1986).

2. **Propositional Content:** Content around a speech act; describes states or events. "The bright student received an A in Mathematics" = two propositions: "student is bright" + "student received A." More propositions → longer reading time (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983).

3. **Thematic Structure:** Good speakers judge what listeners do/don't know; track where they are leading listeners; make accurate judgments of listener's current understanding.

**Five stages of speech production:**
1. **Discourse plans** — decide kind of discourse (conversation, description, instructions, joke).
2. **Sentence plans** — select specific sentences; determine speech act, propositional content, thematic structure.
3. **Constituent plans** — plan components of sentence; pick words, phrases, put in right order.
4. **Articulatory program** — coordinated sequence of muscular contractions in/about the mouth.
5. **Articulation** — actual output of speech.

**Gricean Maxims (Grice, 1975):** Quantity, Quality, Relation, Manner. Violation = conversational implicature.

**Speech errors:** "Slips are seldom 'illegal' combinations of sounds; morphemes tend to slip as entire units" (Clark & Clark, 1977). "Bloopers": "homely friendmaker" for "Friendly Homemaker Program."

**Transformational Rules:** Chomsky (1965, 1975): Deep structure (underlying linguistic structure, specifies meaning) vs. Surface structure (actual spoken/read form). Synonymous sentences = same deep structure, different surface structures. Ambiguous sentences = different deep structures, same surface structure.

**Key terms for marks:** Clark & Clark (1977); speech acts; propositional content; thematic structure; Searle (1969) indirect speech acts; five stages of speech production; Gricean Maxims (Grice, 1975); conversational implicature; slips of the tongue; bloopers; deep vs. surface structure; transformational rules (Chomsky, 1965, 1975).

### Flashcard 32 — Aphasia / Speech Disorders

**FRONT:** What is aphasia? Distinguish between Broca's and Wernicke's aphasia. What are the main categories of speech disorders?

**BACK:**

**Aphasia:** "An impairment of language functioning caused by damage to the left hemisphere of the brain" (Garrett, 2003; Hillis & Caramazza, 2003).

**Types of aphasia:**
- **Broca's aphasia:** Damage to premotor area. Speech contains excess pauses and slips; trouble finding words; fails to use function words (a, the, of) → agrammatic sentences. Verbal comprehension largely preserved.
- **Wernicke's aphasia:** Damage to left temporal lobe. Impaired understanding of spoken words. Speech is fluent phonetically/syntactically but semantically incoherent; neologisms (nonsense words).
- **Global aphasia:** Highly impaired comprehension AND production. Caused by lesions to both Broca's and Wernicke's areas. 32% of aphasias post-stroke.
- **Anomic aphasia:** Difficulties in naming objects/retrieving words.

**Speech disorders (Roseberry-McKibbin, 1995):** Three underlying categories: voice, articulation, fluency.
1. **Voice disorders:** Incorrect phonation (breathy, strained, hoarse) or resonation (hyper-/hypo-nasality).
2. **Speech sound disorders:** (a) Articulation/phonetic disorders — difficulty physically producing sounds (structural abnormalities, faulty learning, nervous system damage); (b) Phonemic disorders — difficulty learning sound distinctions.
3. **Fluency disorders:** Disrupted pauses/hesitations; cluttered (rapid, extra sounds), stuttered (repeated/blocked at word beginnings).
4. **Apraxia of speech:** (See Flashcard 33.)
5. **Dysprosody:** Alterations in intensity, timing, rhythm, cadence, intonation. Cause: neurological pathologies.
6. **Dysarthria:** Weakness/paralysis of speech muscles from nerve/brain damage. Causes: strokes, Parkinson's, ALS.

**Other language disorders:** Autism (echolalia, Kanner 1943); Alzheimer's (semantic simplification); SLI (deficit in function morphemes, ~50% develop dyslexia).

**Key terms for marks:** aphasia; Broca's area (premotor area); Wernicke's area (left temporal lobe); agrammatism; neologisms; global aphasia; anomic aphasia; echolalia; voice/articulation/fluency triad; phonetic vs. phonemic disorders; dysprosody; dysarthria; SLI; DAF.

### Flashcard 33 — Dyspraxia

**FRONT:** What is developmental dyspraxia? How does it differ from apraxia of speech? What are its symptoms and causes?

**BACK:**

**Developmental dyspraxia (Dewey, 1995):** A disorder characterised by impairment in the ability to plan and carry out sensory and motor tasks. Individuals "appear 'out of sync' with their environment."

**Symptoms (variable):** Poor balance and coordination, clumsiness, vision problems, perception difficulties, emotional and behavioural problems, difficulty with reading/writing/speaking, poor social skills, poor posture, poor short-term memory.

**Intelligence:** May be of average or above average intelligence but behave immaturely (Henderson & Henderson, 2003). Lifelong disorder.

**Apraxia of speech / verbal apraxia / dyspraxia:** Speech disorder — trouble saying what one wants to say correctly and consistently.

**Two main types:**
1. **Acquired apraxia of speech:** Any age (typically adults); caused by brain damage (stroke, head injury, tumour). May co-occur with dysarthria (muscle weakness) or aphasia (language impairment).
2. **Developmental apraxia of speech (DAS):** Present from birth; affects more boys than girls. Also called: developmental verbal apraxia, developmental verbal dyspraxia, articulatory apraxia, childhood apraxia of speech. Differs from developmental delay (follows different path, not just slower).

**Causes of DAS:** Not yet known. Some believe disorder related to overall language development; others believe neurological disorder affecting brain's signals to speech muscles. Brain imaging has not found specific lesions. Family history of communication disorders/learning disabilities suggests genetic factors (Kasper et al., 2005).

**Symptoms:** Difficulty putting sounds/syllables in correct order; inconsistent mistakes; "groping" for right sound/word; incorrect prosody (rhythms, stresses, inflections).

**Treatment:** Occupational therapy, speech therapy, cueing/communication forms.

**Key terms for marks:** developmental dyspraxia; Dewey (1995); "out of sync"; lifelong disorder; Henderson & Henderson (2003); apraxia of speech; acquired vs. developmental (DAS); prosody errors; "groping"; Kasper et al. (2005) genetic factors.

### Flashcard 34 — Multilingualism and Thinking

**FRONT:** What is multilingualism? How does it affect cognition and thinking? Distinguish additive from subtractive bilingualism. What cognitive benefits have been documented?

**BACK:**

**Multilingualism:** Knowledge of more than one language by a person or community. Bloomfield (1933): should have native-like competence in all. Modern broader definition (Haugen, 1953; Grosjean, 1982): uses languages regularly and switches where necessary, regardless of symmetry of command.

**Types:**
- **Elite** (learned in formal setting) vs. **Neighbourhood** (acquired through interaction).
- **Simultaneous** (two languages from birth) vs. **Sequential** (one first, then another).
- **Additive bilingualism (Cummins, 1976):** Second language acquired in addition to well-developed first → positive cognitive effects.
- **Subtractive bilingualism:** Second language elements replace first → negative effects.

**Threshold effect:** Individuals may need high competence in both languages for positive effects.

**Multilingualism and cognition (Cummins, 1976):** Additive → increased thinking ability; subtractive → decreased thinking ability.

**Documented cognitive benefits:**
1. Keener awareness; enhanced metalingual abilities (Bialystok, 2001).
2. Better ability to manipulate language for thinking and problem solving (Cummins, 1981).
3. Better able to deal with distractions; offset age-related declines (Bialystok et al., 2004).
4. Better verbal and spatial abilities (Diaz, 1983).
5. Greater cognitive flexibility, better problem solving, higher-order thinking (Hakuta, 1986).
6. Better conceptual development, creativity, analogical reasoning (Diaz, 1985).
7. Divergent thinking and figural creativity (Landry, 1968–1974).
8. Competent multilinguals scored highest on analogy-solving (Eleanor, 1993).

**Code-switching:** Ability to select from either language mid-utterance. Matrix language provides scaffolding; embedded language fills gaps (Myers Scotton, 1993). MacSwan (1997, 1999): minimalist approach — nothing constrains code switching apart from requirements of mixed grammars.

**Neural mechanisms:** fMRI findings contradictory. Early bilinguals build adaptable network integrating later-acquired languages. Late bilinguals must establish new neural areas.

**Key terms for marks:** Bloomfield (1933); Haugen (1953); Grosjean (1982, 1985); additive/subtractive bilingualism (Cummins, 1976); threshold effect; code-switching; matrix language; Myers Scotton (1993); Bialystok (2001, 2004); Hakuta (1986); Diaz (1983, 1985); Landry; early vs. late bilinguals; fMRI.

### Flashcard 35 — Second Language Acquisition

**FRONT:** What factors affect second language acquisition? Is there a critical period for L2? How does L2 acquisition differ from L1?

**BACK:**

**General:** Languages learnt at different ages, situations, competence levels. "Passive" exposure during childhood can lead to competence reactivated later (Ellis, 1995; Franceschini, 1996, 1999).

**Age factor:** Some aspects (vocabulary comprehension, fluency) acquired just as well after adolescence as before. Mastery of nativelike pronunciation depends on early acquisition. "There do not appear to be critical periods for second-language acquisition" (Birdsong, 1999). Possible exception: native accent.

**Adults vs. children:** Adults may appear to have harder time because they retain native language as dominant. Young children typically must switch dominant language (e.g., schooling in new language) → learn new language to higher mastery.

**L2 learning process:** "A series of cognitive procedures by which the learner progressively constructs the grammar of the new language," assisted by LAD (more for L1, less for L2) and LASS (Bruner, 1982, 1983) and Vygotskyan scaffolding (Vygotsky, 1978).

**Differential difficulty:** Depends on shared roots and inflection. E.g., easier for native English speaker to acquire Spanish than Russian (English/Spanish share more roots; Russian more inflected). "The difficulty of learning a language as a second language does not appear to have much to do with its difficulty as a first language."

**Single-system vs. dual-system:** See Flashcard 30. Early bilinguals build adaptable network; late bilinguals establish new neural areas.

**Key terms for marks:** Age factor; Birdsong (1999); critical period for L2 (absent except native accent); passive exposure; Vygotsky (1978); Bruner (1982, 1983); LAD (more for L1, less for L2); LASS; differential difficulty; shared roots; inflection.

### Flashcard 36 — Learning Disability (Dyslexia)

**FRONT:** What is dyslexia? Describe the types and functional subtypes. How is it related to other language disorders?

**BACK:**

**Definition (1968 World Federation of Neurologists):** "A disorder in children who, despite conventional classroom experience, fail to attain the language skills of reading, writing, and spelling commensurate with their intellectual abilities." Not due to mental retardation, brain damage, or lack of intelligence. Caused by impairment in brain's ability to translate images from eyes/ears into understandable language. Average-to-superior intelligence. More common in boys. Earlier treatment → more favourable outcome (Schulte-Körne, Warnke & Remschmidt, 2006).

**Warning signs:** Letter and number reversals (Birsh, 2005); difficulty copying; uncoordinated; difficulty with left/right; hand dominance not established. Auditory problems: difficulty remembering/understanding what is heard; missing parts of words; words sound funny.

**Types (Heim, Tschierse & Amunts, 2008):**
- **Primary dyslexia:** Dysfunction of left side of brain (cerebral cortex); does not change with age; rarely read above fourth-grade level; hereditary.
- **Secondary/developmental dyslexia:** Caused by hormonal development during early fetal development; diminishes as child matures (Galaburda & Cestnick, 2003).

**Functional subtypes:**
- **Visual dyslexia:** Number/letter reversals; inability to write symbols in correct sequence.
- **Auditory dyslexia:** Difficulty with sounds of letters/groups of letters; sounds perceived as jumbled.
- **Dysgraphia:** Difficulty holding/controlling pencil for correct markings (Facoetti et al., 2003).

**Psychosocial effects:** Frustration → withdrawal, depression, low self-esteem; strained peer/sibling interactions; dislike of school.

**Relationship to other disorders:** SLI → dyslexia link: ~50% SLI children develop dyslexia (Bishop & Snowling, 2004). Language-based learning disabilities involve problems with reading, spelling, writing.

**Key terms for marks:** dyslexia; World Federation of Neurologists (1968); primary dyslexia (hereditary, left-cortex dysfunction); secondary/developmental dyslexia (hormonal; Galaburda & Cestnick, 2003); visual dyslexia; auditory dyslexia; dysgraphia (Facoetti et al., 2003); letter/number reversals (Birsh, 2005); SLI→dyslexia link (Bishop & Snowling, 2004); average-to-superior intelligence.

### Flashcard 37 — Gardners Eight Criteria for Multiple Intelligences (Bonus Card)

**FRONT:** What are Gardner's eight criteria for identifying a candidate intelligence? How do they inform the assessment of each proposed intelligence?

**BACK:**

A capacity had to meet **eight criteria** (drawn from biological sciences, logical analysis, developmental psychology, experimental psychology, and psychometrics):

1. **Brain isolation by brain damage** — potential for selective impairment of the capacity through brain damage.
2. **Evolutionary history** — evidence the capacity has an evolutionary basis.
3. **Core operations** — identifiable core operations (e.g., pitch discrimination for musical intelligence).
4. **Susceptibility to encoding** — the capacity can be encoded in a symbol system.
5. **Developmental progression** — clear developmental trajectory with distinct stages.
6. **Idiot-savants, prodigies, exceptional people** — existence of individuals with extraordinary abilities in the domain alongside deficits elsewhere.
7. **Experimental psychology support** — findings from experimental studies support the capacity.
8. **Psychometric support** — psychometric tests can assess the capacity.

These criteria collectively argue for treating each intelligence as a relatively autonomous capacity, not merely a sub-skill of general intelligence ("g").

**Key terms for marks:** eight criteria; brain isolation by brain damage; evolutionary history; core operations; susceptibility to encoding; developmental progression; idiot-savants/prodigies; experimental psychology; psychometrics; relatively autonomous capacity.
