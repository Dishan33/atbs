🏗️ Foundations of Cognitive Psychology 6 theories ▾
Cognitive Psychology: Meaning & Definition
William James (1890) · Neisser (1967)
Defined as "the scientific study of the mind." Study of mental processes — perceiving, learning, remembering, thinking. Internal mental processes are real psychological phenomena, not just intervening variables (contra behaviourists).
Key terms
  • scientific study of the mind
  • empiricists vs. nativists
  • William James (1890)
  • Tolman (1932) cognitive map
  • Bartlett (1932) schema
  • six forces of neocognitive revolution
  • twelve domains
History of Cognitive Psychology
Aristotle vs Plato (ancient) · British Empiricists (18th C.) · Wundt/Titchener vs Brentano (19th C.) · Tolman (1932) · Bartlett (1932) · Neocognitive Revolution (1950s)
Three periods: early thoughts, Renaissance, modern. Aristotle — knowledge in heart; Plato — in brain. Empiricists — three types of internal representation. Tolman (1932) — cognitive map. Bartlett (1932) — schema. 1950s revival: six forces.
12 Domains of Cognitive Psychology
Modern consensus
Cognitive Neuroscience, Perception, Pattern Recognition, Attention, Consciousness, Memory, Knowledge Representation, Imagery, Language, Developmental Psychology, Thinking & Concept Formation, Human & Artificial Intelligence.
Research Methods in Cognitive Psychology
Various
4 methods: (1) Laboratory/controlled experiments — IV/DV/control variables, probable causality; (2) Psychobiological research — 3 categories; (3) Self-reports/case studies/naturalistic observation — hypothesis formulation; (4) Computer simulations & AI. Cognitive science = cross-disciplinary.
Cognitive Neuroscience
Various (21st century convergence)
Every cognitive process supported by electrochemical brain processes. MRI, PET, EEG show structures and processes. Karl Lashley (1929) — localisation vs. distributed processing.
Bloom's Taxonomy of Cognitive Domain
Bloom (1956/1965) · Anderson & Krathwohl (2000)
3 domains (cognitive, psychomotor, affective). 6 cognitive levels: Knowledge → Comprehension → Application → Analysis → Synthesis → Evaluation. Anderson & Krathwohl (2000) reverse top two. Hummel & Huitt (1994) confirmed first 4 hierarchy.
⚙️ Information Processing 2 theories ▾
Principles of Information Processing
Neisser (1967) · Huitt (2000)
4 principles: (1) Limited capacity & bottlenecks (Broadbent, 1975; Case, 1978); (2) Executive function/control mechanism (Atkinson & Shiffrin, 1971); (3) Bottom-up + top-down two-way flow; (4) Genetic/biological preparedness.
Key terms
  • Neisser (1967)
  • computational metaphor
  • bottlenecks
  • executive function
  • bottom-up vs. top-down
  • biological preparedness
Sternberg's Information Processing Approach
Sternberg (1988)
Development = skills-based and continuous. 3 components: Metacomponents (executive/planning), Performance Components (executing strategies), Knowledge-Acquisition Components (learning new info). Part of larger Triarchic Theory. Links to Bruner & Vygotsky.
🧩 Memory 8 theories ▾
Atkinson & Shiffrin Model of Memory
Atkinson & Shiffrin (1968)
Stage theory. 3 stages: Sensory Register (Sperling, 1960) → STS/working memory (5 ± 2, maintenance vs. elaborate rehearsal) → LTS (episodic/semantic, Tulving 1972). Memory ≠ Store. Direct pathway from sensory register to LTS also postulated.
Three Storage Systems (Sensory–STM–LTM)
Miller (1956) · broad consensus
Sensory: transduction, exact-copy, <½ s (vision), ~3 s (hearing). STM: 7 ± 2 items (Miller, 1956), 3–20 s, decay, chunking, iconic/acoustic/working. LTM: meaning + importance, schemas, top-down processing, encoding/consolidation.
Waugh & Norman's Primary/Secondary Memory
Waugh & Norman (1965)
First modern behavioural model. PM (short-term, displacement, verbatim recall) vs. SM (longer-term, requires rehearsal). 16-digit probe task: interference > decay. "Boxes in the head" metaphor.
Levels of Processing (Craik & Lockhart)
Craik & Lockhart (1972) · Craik & Tulving (1975)
Reaction against "boxes in the head." Shallow sensory → deeper semantic. Deeper = better retention. Maintenance vs. elaborate rehearsal. 3 criticisms (Baddeley, 1978): mundane, untestable, circular. Self-reference effect (Rogers et al., 1977).
Self-Reference Effect
Rogers, Kuiper & Kirker (1977)
Self-reference words recalled best over structural, phonemic, semantic conditions. Self-schema = "I, me, mine" (Bellezza, 1992). Rich internal network for self-information.
Level of Recall (LOR)
P. I. Zinchenko (1962, 1981)
Words encoded by deep means retained better. Logical-connection > concrete > no-meaning. "Level of recall is determined by the goal of action." Published in Russian — under-recognised.
Memory & Brain / Cellular Bases
Squire (1987) · Scoville & Milner (1953) · Hebb (1949) · Haxby & Ungerleider (1996) · Schacter et al. (1996)
Case H.M. (1953): bilateral hippocampal removal → anterograde amnesia, normal STM. Case R.B.: CA1 pyramidal cells. Hebb's Law (1949): co-activation strengthens synapses. LTP. NMDA receptor + glutamate + AP5. HERA model. Implicit vs. explicit retrieval = separate brain systems.
Memory Consolidation
Squire (1987)
Biological changes underlying long-term retention. Hippocampus coordinates consolidation but is NOT the storage repository — effects take place in neocortex. Temporally limited retrograde amnesia. Korsakoff's syndrome: dorsomedial thalamus + mammillary bodies.
💡 Intelligence 8 theories ▾
Alfred Binet & IQ
Binet & Simon (1905) · Stern (1912/1914) · Terman (1916)
Binet-Simon Scale (1905): 30 items, 50 children. IQ = (MA/CA) × 100 (Stern). Stanford-Binet (Terman, 1916). Five components: abstract reasoning, comprehension, clear direction of thought, purposeful thinking, self-corrective judgment.
Spearman's Two-Factor Theory
Spearman (1904) · revised 1927
g factor ("mental energy") + s factors. Group factors: verbal, numerical, mental speed. Method: tetrad difference. Critics: Thorndike (1926, three intelligences), Thomson (1939, sampling theory), Thurstone (1935, seven primary abilities). Carroll (1993): Stratum III.
Guilford's Structure of Intellect (SOI)
Guilford (1956; modified 1977)
3 dimensions: Contents (5) × Operations (5) × Products (6) = 150 cells. Originally 4 × 5 × 6 = 120. Cube representation. Rejected "g." Criticisms (Eysenck, 1972): restricted range, orthogonal rotation, too narrow.
Gardner's Multiple Intelligences
Gardner (1983)
8 (or 9) intelligences. Eight criteria. Bio-psychological potential. Bodily-kinesthetic = use whole body to solve problems. Personal intelligences. Criticisms: Morgan (1996), Hunt (2001), Eysenck (1990).
Bodily-Kinesthetic Intelligence
Gardner (1983)
Potential to use whole body/parts of body to solve problems. Mental abilities to coordinate bodily movements. Learn by muscular movement/doing. Careers: athletes, dancers, actors, surgeons, builders, police, soldiers.
Sternberg's Triarchic Theory
Sternberg (1985)
3 subtheories: Componential/Analytical (metacomponents, performance, knowledge-acquisition), Experiential/Creative (novelty + automation), Contextual/Practical (adaptation, shaping, selection = "street smarts"). Successful intelligence = balance. Criticisms: Gottfredson (2003), Das (2004).
PASS Theory of Intelligence
Das, Nagliery & Kirby (1994)
PASS = Planning, Attention-Arousal, Simultaneous, Successive. Grounded in Luria's three functional units. CAS = psychometric instrument. Simultaneous = surveyability (occipital-parietal). Successive = serial order (fronto-temporal).
✨ Creativity 3 theories ▾
Creativity: Meaning, Aspects, Stages
Wallas (1926) · Guilford (1986) · Torrance (1966, 1974) · Newell, Shaw & Simon (1963) · Hennessey & Amabile (1988)
"Appropriate novelty." Four criteria (Newell et al., 1963). Guilford: divergent thinking (fluency, flexibility, originality, elaboration). Torrance TTCT: six components. Wallas: Preparation → Incubation → Illumination → Verification. little-c vs. Big-C (Beghetto & Kaufman, 2007).
Investment & Confluence Theory
Sternberg (2006)
6 resources: intellectual abilities (synthetic/analytic/practical-contextual), knowledge, thinking styles (legislative), personality (risk-taking, ambiguity tolerance), motivation (intrinsic; Amabile, 1983), environment. "Buy low and sell high." Confluence: thresholds, partial compensation, multiplicative interaction.
Creativity and Intelligence (Relationship)
Terman (1925) · Guilford (1967) · Torrance (1977) · Hayes (1989)
Terman: 1,528 gifted children (IQ 151) — none noteworthy creativity. Torrance: r = .20. Threshold hypothesis (Guilford, IQ ≥ 120 necessary not sufficient). Triangularity hypothesis. Certification hypothesis (Hayes).
🧩 Problem Solving 6 theories ▾
Problem Solving: Approaches & Strategies
Various · Newell, Shaw & Simon (1958)
4 approaches: Traditional (S-R), Gestalt (insight/restructuring), Information Processing (GPS; problem space, operators), Modelling (domain + task model). Master strategies: algorithm (guarantees) vs. heuristic (rules of thumb).
Gestalt Approach to Problem Solving
Kohler (1925) · Wertheimer (1959)
Kohler (1925): reorganisation of objects; flash of insight. Reproductive (rote) vs. Productive (insight/creativity) thinking. Nine-dot problem = restructuring. Sagacity. Einstellung (mental set) and functional fixedness are Gestalt-originated obstacles.
Well-Defined vs. Ill-Defined Problems
Greeno (1978)
Well-defined (clear problem + info; e.g., math). Ill-defined (unclear; e.g., "how to bring peace"). Greeno: arrangement, inducing-structure, transformation problems (Tower of Hanoi). 16 characteristics of difficult problems.
Techniques of Problem Solving
Wickelgren (1974) · Ericsson & Simon · Newell (1977)
Generate & Test, Means-Ends Analysis, Backward Search, Planning/Analogy, Thinking Aloud (Ericsson & Simon). Wickelgren's 7: Inference, Classification, State Evaluation & Hill Climbing, Subgoals, Contradiction, Working Backward, Finding Relations. IDEAL (Bransford & Stein).
Functional Fixedness
Duncker (1935) · Simmel (1953)
Tendency to see objects as having only one typical use. Candle problem: 43% solved (boxes as containers) vs. 100% (boxes empty). Simmel's coin problem: 3+3+2 grouping. Cognitive barrier; remedy = reorganisation.
Environmental & Cultural Blocks
Textbook synthesis
Environmental (8): management style, distractions, physical discomfort, lack of support, stress, communication, monotonous work, expectations. Cultural (6): status quo, dislike of change, fantasy/humour, intuition unreliable, competition/cooperation, taboos.
🗣️ Language 7 theories ▾
Language Acquisition: Stages & Building Blocks
Skinner (1957) · Chomsky · Piaget · Bruner (1966, 1968) · Lenneberg (1967)
Building blocks: phonemes, morphemes, syntax, semantics, pragmatics. Stages: cooing → babbling → holophrases → telegraphic speech → adult sentences. 4 theories: Behaviourist (Skinner), Innateness (Chomsky), Cognitive (Piaget), Interactionist (Bruner). Critical period. Williams Syndrome.
Chomsky's Innateness Theory
Chomsky (1957, 1959, 1965, 1975)
LAD, Universal Grammar, Principles and Parameters. Slobin: process mechanism. Evidence: vocal tract, Broca's/Wernicke's, creoles, Nicaraguan signing. Limitations: theoretical only; Jim case (Bard & Sachs, 1977).
Single-System vs. Dual-System Hypotheses
Hernandez et al. (2001) · De Houwer (1995) · Paradis (1981) · Ojemann & Whitaker (1978)
Single-system: one brain region. Dual-system: separate. Bilingual aphasia evidence equivocal. Cortical stimulation: "some aspects singly, others separately." Concept Mediation (Kroll & Sholl, 1992). Early vs. late bilinguals.
Clark & Clark: Three Elements of Language Expression
Clark & Clark (1977)
Three elements: Speech Acts (Searle, 1969), Propositional Content, Thematic Structure. Five stages: discourse → sentence → constituent plan → articulatory program → articulation. Gricean Maxims. Deep vs. surface structure (Chomsky, 1965, 1975).
Aphasia / Speech Disorders
Garrett (2003) · Hillis & Caramazza (2003)
Broca's (premotor area, agrammatism), Wernicke's (left temporal, fluent/incoherent, neologisms), Global (both), Anomic. Speech disorders: voice, articulation, fluency. Apraxia, dysprosody, dysarthria. Autism (echolalia). SLI (~50% → dyslexia).
Dyspraxia
Dewey (1995) · Kasper et al. (2005)
Impairment in planning/executing sensory and motor tasks. "Out of sync." Lifelong. Apraxia of speech: acquired vs. developmental (DAS). Prosody errors. Genetic factors (Kasper et al., 2005).
Multilingualism and Thinking
Cummins (1976, 1981) · Bloomfield (1933) · Grosjean (1982)
Additive (positive) vs. subtractive (negative) bilingualism. Threshold effect. Benefits: metalingual awareness (Bialystok), cognitive flexibility (Hakuta), creativity (Diaz), divergent thinking (Landry). India: Three Language Formula.
Second Language Acquisition
Birdsong (1999) · Vygotsky (1978) · Bruner (1982, 1983)
No critical periods for L2 except native accent (Birdsong, 1999). Nativelike pronunciation requires early acquisition. L2 = cognitive procedures + LAD (less for L2) + LASS + Vygotskyan scaffolding. Differential difficulty.
Learning Disability (Dyslexia)
World Federation of Neurologists (1968) · Heim et al. (2008)
Primary dyslexia (left-cortex, hereditary). Secondary/developmental (hormonal). Functional subtypes: visual, auditory, dysgraphia. SLI → dyslexia link (~50%; Bishop & Snowling, 2004).
