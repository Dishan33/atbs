### THEME 1: FOUNDATIONS OF COGNITIVE PSYCHOLOGY

| # | Theory / Model | Theorist(s) & Year | Components | Revision Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Cognitive Psychology: Meaning & Definition** | William James (1890, *Principles of Psychology*); Neisser (1967) | Study of mental processes — perceiving, learning, remembering, thinking; also pattern recognition, attention, consciousness, imagery, language, reasoning | Defined as "the scientific study of the mind." Internal mental processes are real psychological phenomena, not just intervening variables (contra behaviourists). |
| 2 | **History of Cognitive Psychology** | Aristotle vs Plato (ancient); British Empiricists (Berkeley, Hume, J. Mill, J.S. Mill — 18th C.); Wundt/Titchener vs Brentano (late 19th C.); Tolman (1932); Bartlett (1932); Neocognitive Revolution (1950s) | Three historical periods: early thoughts, Renaissance, modern; six forces of the neocognitive revolution | Aristotle — knowledge in heart; Plato — in brain. Empiricists — three types of internal representation (sensory, stored copies, transformations). Tolman (1932) — cognitive map. Bartlett (1932) — schema. 1950s revival due to: failure of behaviourism, communication theory, linguistics, memory research, computer science, Piaget. |
| 3 | **12 Domains of Cognitive Psychology** | Modern consensus (textbook synthesis) | 12 areas: Cognitive Neuroscience, Perception, Pattern Recognition, Attention, Consciousness, Memory, Knowledge Representation, Imagery, Language, Developmental Psychology, Thinking & Concept Formation, Human & Artificial Intelligence | Each area is a distinct research field; together they form the architecture of cognitive psychology as a discipline. |
| 4 | **Research Methods in Cognitive Psychology** | Various | 4 methods: (1) Laboratory/controlled experiments, (2) Psychobiological research, (3) Self-reports/case studies/naturalistic observation, (4) Computer simulations & AI | Experiments: IV/DV/control variables, probable causality. Psychobiological: 3 categories (post-mortem, brain imaging of deficits, imaging during normal cognition). Self-reports: hypothesis *formulation*. Computer simulations: model entire cognitive architecture. Cognitive science = cross-disciplinary field. |
| 5 | **Cognitive Neuroscience** | Various (21st century convergence) | Brain imaging techniques (MRI, PET, EEG); every cognitive process supported by electrochemical brain processes; neurocognitive techniques allow seeing into the brain | Every part of cognition from sensation to memory is supported by electrochemical processes in the brain. Neurocognitive imagery (MRI, PET, EEG) shows structures and processes. Karl Lashley (1929) — debated localisation vs. distributed processing. |
| 6 | **Bloom's Taxonomy of Cognitive Domain** | Benjamin Bloom (1956/1965); revised by Anderson & Krathwohl (2000) | 3 domains (cognitive, psychomotor, affective); 6 cognitive levels: Knowledge → Comprehension → Application → Analysis → Synthesis → Evaluation | Each level progressively more complex; requires greater elaboration. First 4 levels form a confirmed hierarchy; Synthesis & Evaluation debate: Anderson & Krathwohl (2000) reverse the top two. Hummel & Huitt (1994) confirmed hierarchy for first 4 levels. |

### THEME 2: INFORMATION PROCESSING

| # | Theory / Model | Theorist(s) & Year | Components | Revision Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | **Principles of Information Processing** | Neisser (1967); Huitt (2000) | 4 principles: (1) Limited capacity & bottlenecks, (2) Executive function / control mechanism, (3) Two-way flow (bottom-up + top-down), (4) Genetic/biological preparedness | Cognition = computational in nature (mind = software, brain = hardware). Links to Bruner (1977, 1986) and Vygotsky (1978) — social context shapes cognition. |

### THEME 3: MEMORY

| # | Theory / Model | Theorist(s) & Year | Components | Revision Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 9 | **Atkinson & Shiffrin Model of Memory (Multi-Store Model)** | Atkinson & Shiffrin (1968) | 3 stages: Sensory Register → Short-Term Store (STS/working memory) → Long-Term Store (LTS) | Most widely used stage theory model. Control processes operate on information as it passes through stores. Attention + automaticity govern sensory register. Maintenance vs. elaborate rehearsal. Direct pathway from sensory register to LTS also postulated. |
| 10 | **Three Storage Systems (Sensory–STM–LTM)** | Miller (1956); broad consensus | Sensory memory (½ s vision, 3 s hearing; exact-copy; transduction); STM (7 ± 2 items; 3–20 s; iconic/acoustic/working; decay-based); LTM (meaning + importance; schemas; top-down processing; encoding/consolidation) | Transduction = energy conversion from environment to electrical brain signals. Miller's Magic Number — chunking extends capacity. STM operations: iconic, acoustic, working memory. LTM = organised into schemas; top-down processing shapes perception and bias. |
| 11 | **Waugh & Norman's Primary/Secondary Memory Model** | Waugh & Norman (1965) | Primary Memory (PM): short-term, verbatim recall, limited capacity, displacement-based loss; Secondary Memory (SM): longer-term, requires rehearsal for transfer | First modern behavioural model of memory. Borrowed from William James's primary/secondary memory dichotomy. 16-digit probe task: forgetting due to interference > decay. PM = "boxes in the head" metaphor. PM holds verbal information for verbatim recall; SM requires rehearsal. |
| 12 | **Levels of Processing Model (Craik & Lockhart)** | Craik & Lockhart (1972); revised by Craik & Tulving (1975); Craik & Watkins (1973); Lockhart, Craik & Jacoby (1975) | Shallow sensory analysis → deeper pattern recognition → deepest semantic/long-term-associations. Maintenance rehearsal vs. elaborate rehearsal | Reaction against "boxes in the head." Deeper processing = better retention. "Spread" of processing, not strict sequence. Three criticisms (Baddeley, 1978): (1) mundane conclusion, (2) untestable, (3) circular. Craik & Tulving (1975): structural < phonological < semantic decision for recognition. Proofreading (shallow semantic) vs. reading for gist (shallow visual + deep semantic). |
| 13 | **Self-Reference Effect** | Rogers, Kuiper & Kirker (1977) | Self-reference recalled best over structural, phonemic, semantic conditions | Self-schema = organised system of internal attributes around "I, me, mine" (Bellezza, 1992). Rich internal network for self-information. Possibly dedicated brain space for self-representation. |
| 14 | **Level of Recall (LOR)** | P. I. Zinchenko (1962, 1981) | Words encoded by deep means retained better; logical-connection condition > concrete > no-meaning | "Level of recall is determined by the goal of an action." Published in Russian, not widely distributed — hence under-recognised. |
| 15 | **Memory & Brain / Cellular Bases** | Squire (1987); Scoville & Milner (1953–1957); Haxby & Ungerleider (1996); Schacter et al. (1996); Hebb (1949) | Encoding (acquisition + consolidation), storage, retrieval. Case H.M. (bilateral medial temporal lobe removal, 1953). Case R.B. (CA1 pyramidal cells). LTP (NMDA receptor, glutamate). HERA model. | H.M.: normal STM, severe anterograde amnesia. Bilateral hippocampal damage → cannot form new long-term memories. LTP = Long-Term Potentiation (Baddeley, 1993). Hebb's Law (1949): repeated co-activation strengthens synapses. NMDA receptor + glutamate + AP5. Memories stored as distributed neocortical representations. |
| 16 | **Memory Consolidation** | Squire (1987); various | Biological changes underlying long-term retention; hippocampus coordinates consolidation but is NOT the storage repository; effects take place in neocortex | Amnesia from medial temporal lobe damage = temporally limited retrograde amnesia (1–few years before damage). Once consolidated, hippocampus no longer required. Diencephalic amnesia: dorsomedial nucleus of thalamus + mammillary bodies (Sergei Korsakoff; alcoholic Korsakoff's syndrome — chronic alcoholism → vitamin deficiency → brain damage). |

### THEME 4: INTELLIGENCE

| # | Theory / Model | Theorist(s) & Year | Components | Revision Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 17 | **Alfred Binet & IQ** | Alfred Binet (1905 — Binet-Simon Scale); Theodore Simon; William Stern (1912/1914 — IQ formula); Lewis Terman (1916 — Stanford-Binet) | Mental age; chronological age; IQ = (MA/CA) × 100. Binet's 5 components: abstract reasoning, comprehension, clear direction of thought, purposeful thinking, self-corrective judgment | Commissioned by French government. 30 problems, 50 children (1905). Innovation: clinical interview, age-graded norms, rank-order items, first estimate of mental age. All subsequent intelligence tests modelled after Binet. |
| 18 | **Spearman's Two-Factor Theory** | Charles Spearman (1904); revised 1927 (*The Abilities of Man*) | g factor (general intellective factor / "mental energy"); s factors (specific to particular tests); group factors (verbal, numerical, mental speed); tetrad difference; five quantitative principles (conative control, fatigue, mental energy, primordial potencies, retentivity) | "No single event in the history of mental testing has proved to be of such momentous importance" — Guilford (1954). Method: correlational analysis + factor analysis (tetrad-difference). Confirmatory support: Jensen (1998), Carroll (1993, Stratum III). Critics: Thorndike (1926, three intelligences), Thomson (1939, sampling theory), Thurstone (1935, seven primary abilities — later found hierarchical with g at top). |
| 19 | **Guilford's Structure of Intellect (SOI)** | J. P. Guilford (1956 first version; modified 1977) | 3 dimensions: Contents (5: visual, auditory, symbolic, semantic, behavioural) × Operations (5: cognition, memory, divergent production, convergent production, evaluation) × Products (6: units, classes, relations, systems, transformations, implications) = **150 cells** (originally 4×5×6 = 120) | Cube representation. Rejected Spearman's g. Open system — newly discovered categories can be added. Criticisms (Eysenck 1972): restricted range of intelligence, orthogonal rotation (ignoring inter-factor correlations), factors too narrow for practical prediction. |
| 20 | **Gardner's Theory of Multiple Intelligences** | Howard Gardner (1983, *Frames of Mind*); naturalist added 1999 | 8 (or 9) intelligences: Linguistic, Logical-Mathematical, Musical, Bodily-Kinesthetic, Spatial, Interpersonal, Intrapersonal, Naturalistic (1999), Existential (considered 9th). Eight criteria for candidate intelligence | Intelligence = "bio-psychological potential to process information that can be activated in a cultural setting to solve problems or create products of value in a culture." Personal intelligences = interpersonal + intrapersonal. Criticisms: Morgan (1996) — cognitive styles, not constructs; Hunt (2001) — not measurable; Eysenck (1990) — different abilities, not intelligences. |
| 21 | **Bodily-Kinesthetic Intelligence** | Howard Gardner (1983) | Potential to use whole body/parts of body to solve problems; ability to use mental abilities to coordinate bodily movements; learn by muscular movement/doing | Careers: athletes, dancers, actors, surgeons, builders, police, soldiers. No specific cortical region named in the syllabus; justified by the general criterion of "brain isolation by brain damage." Distinguished from spatial intelligence (wide vs. confined spaces). |
| 22 | **Sternberg's Triarchic Theory of Intelligence** | Sternberg (1985, *Beyond IQ*) | 3 subtheories: (1) Componential/Analytical (metacomponents, performance components, knowledge-acquisition components), (2) Experiential/Creative (novelty + automation), (3) Contextual/Practical (adaptation, shaping, selection) | "Successful intelligence" = balance of analytical, creative, and practical abilities. Central feature = adaptability (Cianciolo & Sternberg, 2004). "Street smarts." Analytic vs practical problems (Neisser et al., 1996). Criticisms: Gottfredson (2003) — unempirical, practical intelligence = task-specific knowledge; Das (2004) — difficult to translate into psychometric instruments. |
| 23 | **PASS Theory of Intelligence** | Das, Nagliery & Kirby (1994); grounded in Luria (1966, 1973, 1980) | 4 systems: Planning (prefrontal cortex), Attention-Arousal (brain stem, reticular activating system), Simultaneous processing (occipital-parietal, surveyability), Successive processing (fronto-temporal, serial order). Plus knowledge base. | Three Lurian functional units: (1) cortical arousal/attention (brain stem), (2) simultaneous/successive processing (occipital-parietal / fronto-temporal), (3) programming/regulation/executive functioning (prefrontal lobes). Cognitive Assessment System (CAS) = psychometric instrument. Simultaneous = surveyability; Successive = serial order (syntax, articulating speech). |

### THEME 5: CREATIVITY

| # | Theory / Model | Theorist(s) & Year | Components | Revision Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 24 | **Creativity: Meaning, Aspects, Stages** | Wallas (1926) four stages; Guilford (1986) divergent thinking; Torrance (1966, 1974) six components; Newell, Shaw & Simon (1963) four criteria; Smith, Ward & Finke (1995) four process characteristics; Sternberg (2006) five commonalities; Beghetto & Kaufman (2007) little-c/Big-C | **Aspects:** fluency, flexibility, originality, elaboration, abstractness of titles, resistance to premature closure (Torrance); sensitivity to problems + redefinition (Guilford). **Stages:** Preparation → Incubation → Illumination → Verification (Wallas, 1926) | Creativity = "appropriate novelty" (Hennessey & Amabile, 1988). Historical creativity (Mona Lisa) vs. everyday creativity. Torrance TTCT six components. Wallas stages: preparation (trial-and-error), incubation (unconscious processing), illumination (insight), verification (testing). Two key characteristics of creative people: clear sense of purpose + store of knowledge/techniques. |
| 25 | **Investment & Confluence Theory of Creativity** | Sternberg (2006) | 6 resources: (1) Intellectual abilities (synthetic + analytic + practical-contextual), (2) Knowledge, (3) Thinking styles (legislative), (4) Personality (risk-taking, ambiguity tolerance, self-efficacy, overcoming obstacles), (5) Motivation (intrinsic, task-focused; Amabile 1983), (6) Environment | "Buy low and sell high" — creative people seek opposition, defy the crowd. Confluence principle: thresholds, partial compensation, multiplicative interaction. Three intellectual skills must all be present: synthetic (new ideas), analytic (evaluate ideas), practical-contextual (sell ideas). |
| 26 | **Creativity and Intelligence (Relationship)** | Terman (1925); Torrance (1977); Guilford (1967); Hayes (1989) | Threshold hypothesis (Guilford, IQ ≥ 120 necessary but not sufficient); Triangularity hypothesis; Certification hypothesis (Hayes); separate factors; median r = .20 (Torrance, 178 correlations) | Terman (1925): 1,528 gifted children (mean IQ 151) — none showed noteworthy creativity. Weak correlation between IQ and TTCT. Certification hypothesis: society requires formal education (correlated with IQ) for creative opportunities → denies low-IQ individuals the chance to display creativity. |

### THEME 6: PROBLEM SOLVING

| # | Theory / Model | Theorist(s) & Year | Components | Revision Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 27 | **Problem Solving: Approaches & Strategies** | Various: traditional (S-R learning); Gestalt; Information Processing (Newell, Shaw & Simon, 1958, GPS); Wickelgren (1974); Newell (1977) | 4 approaches: (1) Traditional/associative learning, (2) Gestalt (insight, restructuring), (3) Information processing & computer simulation, (4) Problem-solving-as-modelling. Two master strategies: algorithm vs heuristic | Traditional: stimulus-response associations; extinction (failure) weakens wrong, reinforcement strengthens correct. IP: GPS uses limited-capacity WM, large LTM, serial processor, heuristics. Problem space hypothesis. Problem-space = set of nodes (states of knowledge), solvers move via operators. |
| 28 | **Gestalt Approach to Problem Solving** | Kohler (1925, *The Mentality of Apes*); Wertheimer (1959) | Insight = sudden discovery of correct solution after incorrect trial-and-error attempts. Reproductive thinking vs. productive thinking. Nine-dot problem. Sagacity | Kohler's apes: reorganisation of objects into new structure. Wertheimer: effective problem solving requires productive thinking + sensitivity to structural requirements + going beyond prior knowledge. Einstellung (mental set) and functional fixedness are Gestalt-originated obstacles. Non-insight vs insight problems: metacognitive assessments accurate for non-insight, not for insight. |
| 29 | **Well-Defined vs. Ill-Defined Problems / Types** | Greeno (1978) | Well-defined (clear problem + info needed; e.g., math equation, jigsaw); Ill-defined (unclear nature + info needed; e.g., "how to bring peace"). Greeno's 3 well-defined types: (1) Arrangement problems (anagrams, jigsaw), (2) Inducing-structure problems (identify relationships), (3) Transformation problems (Tower of Hanoi; means-end analysis) | Characteristics of difficult problems: intransparency, commencement opacity, continuation opacity, polytely (multiple goals), inexpressiveness, opposition, transience, complexity, enumerability, connectivity, heterogeneity, dynamics, temporal constraints, temporal sensitivity, phase effects, dynamic unpredictability. |
| 30 | **Techniques of Problem Solving** | Ericsson & Simon; Newell (1977); Wickelgren (1974); Schooler, Ohlsson & Brooks (1993); Heidegger (1968); Bransford & Stein (IDEAL) | Specific techniques: Generate & Test, Means-Ends Analysis, Backward Search, Planning/Analogy, Thinking Aloud. Other strategies: Abstraction, Divide & Conquer, Hypothesis Testing, Lateral Thinking, Method of Focal Objects, Reduction, Research, Root Cause Analysis, Trial-and-Error, Brainstorming. Wickelgren's 7 techniques (Inference, Classification, State Evaluation & Hill Climbing, Subgoals, Contradiction, Working Backward, Finding Relations). | Concurrent vs. retrospective verbalisation (Ericsson & Simon). Protocol analysis → problem behaviour graph (Newell, 1977). Schooler, Ohlsson & Brooks (1993): verbalisation interferes with insight problems — non-reportable/unconscious processes. Expert systems: knowledge base + inference rules + search engine + user interface. Heidegger (1968): essence of thinking not reducible to computing. |
| 31 | **Functional Fixedness** | Duncker (1935); Simmel (1953) | Tendency to see objects as having only a single, typical use (prototypical function). Duncker's candle problem: 43% solved (boxes as containers) vs. 100% solved (boxes empty). Simmel's coin problem: 3 groups of 3, 3, and 2 | Functional fixedness = cognitive barrier; lack of transfer skills. Obvious/available property of an object is the first thing we think of; people keep returning to it. Remedy: reorganisation of the problem. |
| 32 | **Environmental & Cultural Blocks** | Various (textbook synthesis) | Environmental blocks (8): management style, distractions, physical discomfort, lack of support, stress, lack of communication, monotonous work, expectations of others. Cultural blocks (6): unquestioning acceptance of status quo, dislike of change, fantasy/humour not productive, feelings/intuition unreliable, over-emphasis on competition or cooperation, taboos | Five barrier types: Perceptual, Emotional, Intellectual, Expressive, Environmental, Cultural. Overcome own blocks by re-learning; overcome others' blocks by learning to sidestep. Cultural blocks = conditioning to accept what is "normal." |

### THEME 7: LANGUAGE

| # | Theory / Model | Theorist(s) & Year | Components | Revision Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 33 | **Language Acquisition: Stages & Building Blocks** | Skinner (1957); Chomsky; Piaget; Bruner (1966, 1968); Lenneberg (1967); Curtiss (1989) | Building blocks: phonemes, morphemes, syntax, semantics, pragmatics. Stages: cooing → babbling → holophrases → telegraphic speech → basic adult sentences (~age 4). 4 theories: Behaviourist (Skinner), Innateness (Chomsky), Cognitive (Piaget), Interactionist (Bruner) | Overextension errors. Telegraphic speech = "want juice." CDS (child-directed speech) scaffolding. Bruner's LASS. Critical period: guaranteed up to age 6, compromised until after puberty, rare thereafter (Newport & Gleitman, 1995). Genie case (1970, age 13). Williams Syndrome: IQ ~50 but fluent language → language independent of general intelligence. |
| 34 | **Chomsky's Innateness Theory** | Noam Chomsky (1957, 1959, 1965, 1975, 1985) | LAD (Language Acquisition Device), Universal Grammar, Principles and Parameters, Critical Period, Species-specific module, Impoverished input | Chomsky's review of Skinner's *Verbal Behaviour* (1959). Children born with innate language faculty. Every language has nouns/verbs, consonants/vowels. Virtuous errors (e.g., "goed"). Slobin's modification: LAD = process mechanism, not set of linguistic categories. Evidence: human vocal tract, Broca's/Wernicke's areas, creole formation (Bickerton), Nicaraguan signing creole (Pinker, 1994). Limitations: theoretical only; no account of interaction; Jim case (Bard & Sachs, 1977). |
| 35 | **Single-System vs. Dual-System Hypotheses** | Hernandez et al. (2001); De Houwer (1995); Paradis (1981); Ojemann & Whitaker (1978) | Single-system: two languages in one brain region. Dual-system: separate representations. Evidence from bilingual aphasia (equivocal), cortical stimulation, fMRI (contradictory) | Early bilinguals build adaptable network integrating later-acquired languages. Late bilinguals establish new neural areas. Concept Mediation account (Kroll & Sholl, 1992): lexical = language-specific stores, semantic = common conceptual store. Paivio (1986): both in language-specific stores. |
| 36 | **Clark & Clark: Three Elements of Language Expression** | Clark & Clark (1977) | Three elements: (1) Speech Acts (Searle, 1969; indirect speech acts), (2) Propositional Content, (3) Thematic Structure. Five stages of speech production: discourse plan → sentence plan → constituent plan → articulatory program → articulation | Gricean Maxims (Grice, 1975): Quantity, Quality, Relation, Manner. Violation = conversational implicature. Slips of the tongue: morphemes slip as entire units; seldom "illegal" combinations. Chomsky (1965, 1975): deep structure vs. surface structure; transformational rules. |

### THEME 8: COGNITIVE/NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDERS

| # | Theory / Model | Theorist(s) & Year | Components | Revision Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 37 | **Aphasia / Speech Disorders** | Garrett (2003); Hillis & Caramazza (2003); Curtiss (1989); Lenneberg (1967); Kanner (1943); Tallal et al. (1985); Bishop & Snowling (2004) | Aphasia types: Broca's (premotor area, agrammatism), Wernicke's (left temporal lobe, fluent but semantically incoherent, neologisms), Global (both areas), Anomic (word-retrieval difficulty). Speech disorders: voice, articulation/phonemic, fluency, apraxia, dysprosody, dysarthria. | 38 | **Dyspraxia** | Dewey (1995); Henderson & Henderson (2003); Alloway & Temple (2007); Kasper et al. (2005) | Developmental dyspraxia: impairment in planning/executing sensory and motor tasks. Lifelong disorder. Apraxia of speech: acquired (brain damage) vs. developmental (DAS/childhood apraxia). Symptoms: groping for sounds, prosody errors, inconsistent mistakes | Individuals "out of sync" with environment. Average-to-above-average intelligence but may behave immaturely. DAS vs. developmental delay: DAS follows different path, not just slower. Genetic factors suggested (Kasper et al., 2005). Treatment: occupational therapy, speech therapy, cueing. |
| 39 | **Learning Disability (Dyslexia)** | World Federation of Neurologists (1968); Heim, Tschierse & Amunts (2008); Galaburda & Cestnick (2003); Bishop & Snowling (2004); Facoetti et al. (2003) | Primary dyslexia (left-cortex dysfunction, hereditary, does not change with age). Secondary/developmental dyslexia (hormonal, diminishes with maturation). Functional subtypes: visual, auditory, dysgraphia. Language-based learning disabilities. Average-to-superior intelligence | Not due to mental retardation or brain damage. Caused by impairment in brain's ability to translate images from eyes/ears into language. More common in boys. SLI → dyslexia link: ~50% SLI children develop dyslexia. |

### THEME 9: MULTILINGUALISM & COGNITION

| # | Theory / Model | Theorist(s) & Year | Components | Revision Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 40 | **Multilingualism and Thinking** | Cummins (1976, 1981); Bloomfield (1933); Grosjean (1982, 1985); Hakuta (1986); Diaz (1983, 1985); Landry (1968–1974); Bialystok (2001, 2004) | Additive vs. subtractive bilingualism (Cummins, 1976).
