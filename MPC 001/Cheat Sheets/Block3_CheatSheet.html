Unit 1: Language Acquisition

Building Blocks

Phonemes (smallest distinguishable units) · Morphemes (smallest meaningful units; "disliked" = dis+lik+ed) · Syntax (rules for arrangement) · Semantics (meaning) · Pragmatics (use in context). Language = generative (infinite messages from finite symbols) + rule-governed.

Stages of Acquisition

StageAgeFeatures
Cooing~2 moVowel sounds; universal (incl. deaf)
Babbling~6 moConsonant+vowel; shifts to own language phonemes (Locke, 1994); deaf infants stop
Holophrases~12 moOne-word utterances; usually nouns; overextension errors (e.g., "doggie" for any 4-legged animal)

Adult structure~4 yrComplex constructions. Vocab: ~300 at 2, ~1000 at 3, adult-like by 10

Chomsky's Innateness Theory

LAD (Language Acquisition Device) = innate faculty. Universal Grammar + Principles & Parameters. Slobin: LAD = process mechanism. Critical period (Lenneberg, 1967): guaranteed up to 6, compromised until after puberty, rare thereafter. Genie case (1970, age 13): isolated, never acquired grammar. Williams Syndrome: IQ ~50 but fluent language.
Evidence: vocal tract adaptation · Broca's/Wernicke's areas · creole formation (Bickerton) · Nicaraguan signing creole (Pinker, 1994).
Limitations: theoretical only · no account of interaction · Jim case (Bard & Sachs, 1977): exposure without interaction insufficient.

Critical Period Biology

Brain: decline in metabolic rate + neuron numbers during early school years; synapses/metabolic rate bottom out around puberty. Children recover language after left-hemisphere damage; adults → permanent aphasia (Curtiss, 1989; Lenneberg, 1967).

Piaget's Cognitive Stages

Sensorimotor (0–2; lacks object permanence) → Preoperational (2–7; language develops; egocentric) → Concrete operational (7–12; conservation; reversibility) → Formal operational (12+; abstract; only 40–60% adults).
Brown (1973): morpheme mastery order = -ing → in/on → plural -s → forms of "to be."

Unit 2: Language Processing

Clark & Clark (1977) — 3 Elements of Expression

Speech Acts — Searle (1969); indirect speech acts; context-dependent meaning (Gibbs, 1986).
Propositional Content — describes states/events; more propositions = longer reading time (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983).
Thematic Structure — good speakers judge what listeners know.

5 Stages of Speech Production (Clark & Clark, 1977)

Discourse plan → Sentence plan → Constituent plan → Articulatory program → Articulation

Gricean Maxims (Grice, 1975)

Quantity (avoid running off) · Quality (don't lie) · Relation (avoid irrelevance) · Manner (avoid ambiguity). Violation = conversational implicature.

Deep vs. Surface Structure (Chomsky, 1965, 1975)

Transformational rules. Deep structure = underlying meaning. Surface structure = actual spoken form. Synonymous sentences = same deep, different surface. Ambiguous = different deep, same surface.

Speech Errors

"Slips seldom illegal combinations; morphemes slip as entire units." Bloopers: "homely friendmaker" for "Friendly Homemaker Program."

Unit 3: Multilingualism & Cognition

Definitions

Bloomfield (1933): native-like competence in all. Modern (Grosjean, 1982): uses languages regularly, switches where necessary. Elite (formal setting) vs Neighbourhood (natural interaction). Simultaneous vs Sequential bilingualism.

Additive vs Subtractive (Cummins, 1976)

Additive: L2 added to well-developed L1 → positive cognitive effects. Subtractive: L2 replaces L1 → negative effects. Threshold effect: high competence in both needed.

Cognitive Benefits

Enhanced metalingual abilities (Bialystok, 2001) · Better problem solving (Cummins, 1981) · Better verbal+spatial (Diaz, 1983) · Greater cognitive flexibility (Hakuta, 1986) · Better creativity/analogical reasoning (Diaz, 1985) · Divergent thinking (Landry, 1968–74) · Competent multilinguals highest on analogy-solving (Eleanor, 1993).

Code-Switching

Matrix language (scaffolding) + embedded language (fills gaps). Myers Scotton (1993): embedded language island. MacSwan (1997, 1999): nothing constrains code switching apart from mixed grammars.

Single vs. Dual System

Single: one brain region (Hernandez et al., 2001). Dual: separate systems (De Houwer, 1995; Paradis, 1981). Bilingual aphasia evidence equivocal. Ojemann & Whitaker (1978): cortical stimulation → "some singly, others separately." Early bilinguals = adaptable network; late = new neural areas.

Second Language Acquisition

No critical period for L2 except native accent (Birdsong, 1999). L2 = cognitive procedures + LAD (less for L2) + LASS + Vygotskyan scaffolding. Differential difficulty depends on shared roots/inflection.

Unit 4: Language & Speech Disorders

Aphasia

Broca's: premotor area; non-fluent, agrammatic; comprehension preserved.
Wernicke's: left temporal lobe; fluent but semantically incoherent; neologisms.
Global: both areas; 32% post-stroke. Anomic: word-retrieval difficulty.

Speech Disorders (Roseberry-McKibbin, 1995)

Voice (phonation/resonation) · Articulation (phonetic vs phonemic) · Fluency (cluttering, stuttering) · Apraxia (acquired vs developmental DAS) · Dysprosody (rhythm/intonation) · Dysarthria (muscle weakness/paralysis).

Other Disorders

Autism (Kanner, 1943): echolalia; ~50% don't develop functional speech.
Alzheimer's: semantic simplification; phonology/syntax minimally affected.
SLI: deficit in function morphemes; ~50% → dyslexia (Bishop & Snowling, 2004).
Dyspraxia (Dewey, 1995): impairment planning/executing motor tasks; lifelong; "out of sync." DAS: developmental apraxia of speech.
Dyslexia (WFN, 1968): reading/writing/spelling disorder; primary (hereditary) vs secondary (hormonal). Subtypes: visual, auditory, dysgraphia.
