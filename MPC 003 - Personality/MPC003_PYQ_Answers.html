1. Determinants of Personality

Biological Factors Determining Personality Development 10 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2025, Dec 2023, Jun 2023, Dec 2019, Dec 2018, Dec 2014, Dec 2013, Jun 2012
  1. Body Build (Somatotypes): Sheldon (1942) classified body types — Ectomorph (thin, introverted, cerebrotonic), Mesomorph (muscular, dominant, somatotonic), Endomorph (round, sociable, viscerotonic). Body build influences social perception and self-concept. Kretschmer's typology similarly linked physique to personality.
  2. Physical Attractiveness: Brislin & Lewis (1968) found attractive individuals receive more positive social feedback, enhancing confidence and extraversion. The "what is beautiful is good" stereotype shapes lifelong self-perception.
  3. Homeostasis: Cannon (1932) — the body's tendency to maintain internal equilibrium. Hormonal imbalances, temperature variations, and metabolic states affect mood, energy levels, and temperamental stability.
  4. Physical Defects/Health: Adler's concept of organ inferiority — physical limitations can lead to compensation (adaptive) or inferiority complex (maladaptive). Chronic illness affects emotional development and social participation. Physical health influences energy, outlook, and interpersonal engagement.
  5. Heredity & Genes: Twin studies (Bouchard, 1990; Minnesota Study) show heritability of ~40-50% for major traits. Eysenck's PEN model proposes biological basis — ARAS for E, limbic system for N, testosterone/MAO for P. Genes provide the reaction range; environment determines where within that range one falls.
  6. Personality = Biological × Psychological × Environmental (multiplicative model). No single factor acts in isolation. Biological factors provide the foundation; psychological and environmental factors shape expression.

Psychological Factors Determining Personality Development 10 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2025, Dec 2024, Dec 2012, Jun 2013, Dec 2016
  1. Intellectual Determinants: Cognitive abilities shape perception, interpretation, and response to situations. Intelligence influences problem-solving, adaptability, and social competence. Higher cognitive functioning enables more nuanced self-concepts and coping strategies. Mental retardation/limitations affect personality development trajectory.
  2. Emotional Balance: Described as "mental health insurance." Emotionally stable individuals (Eysenck's low N) handle stress better, maintain relationships, and make consistent decisions. Emotional instability leads to anxiety, mood swings, and maladaptive behaviors. Emotional maturity fosters resilience.
  3. Catharsis (Emotional Release): Freud's hydraulic model — pent-up emotions need release. Expression through art, conversation, or physical activity prevents tension accumulation. Inability to cathart leads to psychological distress and personality rigidification. "Bottling up" emotions → eventual eruption or psychosomatic symptoms.
  4. Self-Disclosure: Jourard (1971) emphasized that revealing one's true self to others is essential for mental health. Self-disclosure builds intimacy, reduces real-ideal self gap, and promotes authentic personality development. Low self-disclosure → isolation, loneliness.
  5. Aspiration & Achievement: Level of aspiration (Hoppe, 1930) — what a person aims for. Repeated success → confidence, high self-esteem. Repeated failure → inferiority complex, "sour grapes" rationalization. Achievement motivation (McClelland, n-Ach) shapes goal-directed personality. Delusion of grandeur vs superiority complex.
  6. Self-Concept: Rogers' real self vs ideal self congruence. Positive self-concept → openness, growth. Incongruence → anxiety, defensiveness. Self-concept develops through interactions with significant others and internalizes societal expectations.

Environmental Factors Influencing Personality Development 6 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2022, Jun 2021, Jun 2015, Jun 2014
  1. Social Acceptance: Acceptance by peers, family, community builds self-esteem and security. Social rejection → isolation, "sour-grapes" attitude, defensive personality patterns.
  2. Social Deprivation: Studies of institutionalized children (Spitz, 1945) show lack of early social stimulation causes developmental delays, emotional blunting, and attachment disorders.
  3. Family Factors: Parenting style (authoritarian/authoritative/permissive), ordinal position (firstborn=responsible; lastborn=rebellious/creative; middle=diplomatic), family size. Identification and imitation of parents. Family emotional climate shapes basic trust/mistrust.
  4. Educational Environment: Schools shape discipline, curiosity, social skills. Teachers as role models. Academic success/failure influences self-efficacy beliefs (Bandura).
  5. Cultural Context: Individualistic cultures → independence, self-expression. Collectivistic cultures → interdependence, conformity. Cultural norms shape which traits are valued and expressed. Watson's extreme environmentalism — though exaggerated, powerfully demonstrates environment's role.
  6. Social acceptance, social deprivation, family, education, and culture form key environmental determinants interacting with biological and psychological factors.

Nature vs Nurture Debate in Personality 10 / 6 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2022 (10m), Dec 2021, Jun 2023 (6m each)
  1. Nature (Biological): Genetic inheritance; twin studies — identical twins raised apart show similar traits. Eysenck's PEN biological basis, evolutionary psychology (Buss). Heritability estimates: ~40-60%. Temperament traits (Thomas & Chess) appear early and are biologically rooted.
  2. Nurture (Environmental): Parenting, culture, peers, education. Bandura's social learning — behavior acquired through observation. Behaviorism (Watson, Skinner) emphasizes environmental conditioning. Environmental influences particularly strong in early childhood.
  3. Interactionist Position (Current Consensus): Neither alone. Personality = gene-environment interaction. Genes provide predisposition (diathesis); environment triggers/suppresses expression. Epigenetics — environment can alter gene expression. Reaction range concept — genes set boundaries, environment determines position within range. For 10 marks, add: Minnesota Twin Study, adoption studies, temperament research.

2. Bandura — Social Cognitive / Social Learning Theory

Albert Bandura's Social Learning Theory 10 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2023, Dec 2021, Dec 2019, Jun 2017, Dec 2016, Dec 2015, Jun 2011
  1. Core Premise: Learning occurs in a social context through observation, imitation, and modeling. People are "both products and producers of their environment." Rejects strict behaviorism — cognition mediates between stimulus and response. Beyond classical and operant conditioning.
  2. Triadic Reciprocal Determinism: Three factors interact continuously — Person (P: cognition, biology), Behavior (B: actions), Environment (E: social/physical context). P↔B↔E. Not linear causation but continuous reciprocal interaction.
  3. Bobo Doll Experiment (1961, 1963): Children observed adult model behaving aggressively toward Bobo doll. Children imitated without direct reinforcement — contradicted behaviorist predictions. Effects stronger with same-sex models, male models, and when model was rewarded.
  4. 4 Steps of Modeling/Observational Learning: (a) Attentional — must attend to model (influenced by model's status, attractiveness, similarity). (b) Retentional — symbolically encode and remember behavior. (c) Reproduction — physically/mentally capable of reproducing. (d) Motivational — must have reason to perform (reinforcement, self-efficacy).
  5. Vicarious Reinforcement: Learning by observing consequences others receive. Model rewarded → behavior imitated. Model punished → behavior avoided.
  6. Self-System & Self-Efficacy: Self-efficacy = belief in one's capability to execute behaviors for desired outcomes. 4 sources: (1) Mastery/enactive experiences (most powerful), (2) Vicarious experiences/social modeling, (3) Social persuasion, (4) Physiological/emotional states. High self-efficacy → greater effort, persistence, resilience.
  7. Self-Regulation: People set standards, observe own behavior, judge against standards, self-reinforce or self-punish. Internal control distinguishes social cognitive theory from behaviorism.
  8. Limitations: Behavior more consistent than theory predicts; biological factors underemphasized; theory not fully unified; developmental stages not clearly specified.

Principles of Observational Learning / Modeling Process 6 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2024, Dec 2018, Dec 2017, Jun 2017, Jun 2016, Jun 2014, Jun 2013, Dec 2012
  1. Observational learning = learning by watching others (models) without direct reinforcement. Bandura's core contribution beyond behaviorism.
  2. Four key processes: (1) Attention — observer must focus on modeled behavior. Influenced by model characteristics (status, attractiveness, similarity) and observer's cognitive abilities. (2) Retention — behavior must be remembered via symbolic coding (images, words). Mental rehearsal strengthens retention. (3) Reproduction — observer must have physical and cognitive ability to replicate. Practice improves accuracy. (4) Motivation — observer must want to perform; determined by anticipated outcomes (vicarious reinforcement/punishment) and self-efficacy beliefs.
  3. Types of models: Live models (actual people), symbolic models (media, books, films), verbal instruction.
  4. Vicarious reinforcement and punishment — observing consequences to model influences whether behavior is adopted. Key to social transmission of values and norms.

Reciprocal Determinism 3 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2022, Jun 2021, Dec 2017, Jun 2012
  1. Bandura's concept that Person (P), Behavior (B), and Environment (E) interact in a triadic, bidirectional relationship. Each influences and is influenced by the others — not linear causation but continuous mutual influence.
  2. Example: A person's thoughts affect their behavior, which changes their environment, which in turn influences their thoughts.
  3. Key implication: "People are both products and producers of their environment" — active human agency, not passive determinism.

Vicarious Learning 3 MARKS

  1. Learning by observing the consequences of others' behavior — no direct personal experience needed. Core of Bandura's social learning theory.
  2. Vicarious reinforcement: seeing a model rewarded → more likely to imitate. Vicarious punishment: seeing a model punished → less likely to imitate.
  3. Explains how people learn complex behaviors (language, social norms, phobias) without trial-and-error — fundamental to social transmission of culture.

3. Behavioral Assessment

Define Behavioral Assessment — Characteristics, Assumptions, Advantages & Limitations 10 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2024, Jun 2022, Jun 2021, Dec 2020, Dec 2016
  1. Definition: Behavioral assessment focuses on observing, describing, and measuring overt behavior in specific situations rather than inferring underlying traits. "Sine qua non" = direct observation. Contrasts with traditional trait-based assessment. Focuses on current behavior, not past or unconscious dynamics.
  2. Key Characteristics: (a) Focus on current behavior in specific situations. (b) Emphasis on situational determinants (S → R → S — stimulus-response-consequence). (c) Multiple assessment methods across settings. (d) Ongoing, not one-time assessment. (e) Treatment integrated with assessment — functional analysis (ABC model: Antecedents → Behavior → Consequences).
  3. Assumptions: (1) Behavior is situation-specific, not cross-situationally consistent. (2) Behavior is learned and can be unlearned. (3) Assessment should sample behavior directly in natural or analogue settings. (4) Observable behavior is the appropriate unit of analysis. (5) Best understood by what precedes (antecedents) and follows (consequences).
  4. Advantages: Direct measurement reduces inference error; context-specific insights; aids intervention design (functional analysis → targeted treatment); objective, quantifiable data; applicable across ages and settings.
  5. Limitations: Time-consuming and costly; observer bias and reactivity (Hawthorne effect); "what to observe" problem; limited sampling may miss infrequent behaviors; may overlook internal cognitive/emotional processes; visible vs invisible observer dilemma (Kazdin, 1982).
  6. Methods: Naturalistic observation, situational tests, behavioral interviews, self-monitoring, role-playing, psychophysiological recording.

Case Study Method 10 / 6 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2023, Dec 2019, Dec 2016, Jun 2014, Jun 2013
  1. Definition: In-depth, intensive study of a single individual (or small group) using multiple data sources — interviews, observation, tests, records, diaries, archival data. Aimed at holistic understanding.
  2. Purpose: (1) Understand the individual holistically. (2) Generate hypotheses for further research. (3) Illustrate theoretical principles. (4) Study rare/unusual phenomena (Phineas Gage, H.M., Genie). (5) Demonstrate therapeutic effectiveness.
  3. Types: (a) Intrinsic — case inherently interesting. (b) Instrumental — serves to understand broader phenomenon. (c) Collective — multiple cases together. (d) Longitudinal — same case over extended period. (e) Retrospective — reconstructing past from records.
  4. Strengths: Rich, detailed data; captures individual complexity; generates hypotheses; useful for rare conditions; holistic understanding; bridges research and practice; idiographic complement to nomothetic research.
  5. Weaknesses: Poor generalizability (n=1); subjective interpretation; retrospective data unreliable; difficult to establish cause-effect; time-intensive; cannot replicate; vulnerable to researcher bias and selective reporting.
  6. Famous examples: Freud's case studies (Anna O., Little Hans, Rat Man), Piaget's study of his children, Luria's "The Mind of a Mnemonist."

Situational Tests — Methods of Behavioral Assessment 6 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2025, Jun 2017
  1. Situational tests place individuals in simulated real-life situations to observe behavior under controlled conditions. First used by OSS (Office of Strategic Services, 1942) for military officer selection.
  2. Characteristics: Natural or contrived situations; observer(s) record specific behaviors; participants often unaware of exact trait measured (reduces faking); standardized scoring rubrics.
  3. Types: Leaderless group discussion, in-basket technique, role-playing exercises, stress interviews. CEI (Character Education Inquiry — Hartshorne, May & Shuttleworth, 1930) tested altruism, honesty, self-control in school settings. Duplicating technique for detecting cheating.
  4. Limitations: Time-consuming and expensive; Hawthorne effect (behaving differently when observed); limited behavior sampling; scoring reliability depends on observer training; ethical concerns (deception, stress); cannot fully replicate real-world complexity; cross-situational consistency often low.

4. Allport — Dispositional / Trait Theory

Allport's Dispositional Theory / Personal Dispositions 10 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2023, Dec 2017, Jun 2012, Jun 2015
  1. Definition of Personality (1937): "Dynamic organisation within the individual of those psychophysical systems that determine his unique adjustment to his environment." Revised (1961): "...determine his characteristic behavior and thought." Two pillars: Individuality + Consistency.
  2. Lexical Approach: Allport & Odbert (1936) identified 17,953 personality-descriptive words from dictionary. Categorized into traits, temporary states, social evaluations, and miscellaneous. Foundation for modern trait taxonomy.
  3. Three Types of Personal Dispositions (Idiographic): (a) Cardinal Traits — single dominating passion defining a life (Gandhi=non-violence, Machiavelli). Rare. (b) Central Traits — ~5-10 core building blocks; traits people mention when describing someone (honest, friendly, aggressive). (c) Secondary Dispositions — situation-specific, less consistent, more like preferences/attitudes.
  4. Common vs Individual Traits: Common traits = dimensions for comparing people (nomothetic). Individual traits = unique pattern within a person (idiographic). Allport emphasized idiographic — "the individual is more important than the group."
  5. 8 Characteristics of Traits (1966): (1) Real, not fictions. (2) More generalized than habits. (3) Dynamic/determinative. (4) Empirically established. (5) Relatively independent. (6) Not moral judgments. (7) Viewed idiographically and nomothetically. (8) Inconsistent acts don't disprove traits.
  6. Two Modes: Opportunistic (reactive, past-oriented, biological) vs Propriate (proactive, future-oriented, psychological). Mature personality = propriate functioning dominant.

Development of Selfhood (Proprium) — Allport 10 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2025, Jun 2025, Jun 2021
  1. Proprium = Allport's term for the self/ego. Not present at birth; develops gradually through seven propriate functions from infancy to adulthood.
  2. Seven Propriate Functions: (1) Bodily Self (0-1 yr): Infant distinguishes own body from environment. "Me" vs "not-me." Sensory experiences. (2) Self-Identity (1-2 yr): Continuity of self across time. Language — use of "I" and "me." (3) Self-Esteem (2-3 yr): Pride in accomplishments. Wanting to do things independently. Negativism of "terrible twos." (4) Self-Extension (3-5 yr): "Mine" — possessions, people, groups incorporated into self. Loyalty, identification. (5) Self-Image (5-12 yr): "Looking-glass self" — awareness of how others perceive. Development of conscience, goals, ideal self. (6) Rational Coper (12+ yr): Self as problem-solver. Logical thinking, planning, applying reason. Cognitive maturity. (7) Propriate Striving (adolescence+): Long-range goals, sense of purpose, unification of personality. "Becoming" not just "being."
  3. Mature Personality — 6 Attributes: (1) Extension of self. (2) Warm relating (intimacy + compassion). (3) Emotional security/self-acceptance. (4) Realistic perception and skills. (5) Self-insight and humor. (6) Unifying philosophy of life.

Functional Autonomy and Mature Personality 10 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2024, Dec 2020, Jun 2017, Dec 2018
  1. Functional Autonomy: Allport's most distinctive concept. Adult motives become independent of childhood origins. "What was once an instrument becomes a master." Behavior originally done for one reason becomes satisfying in itself.
  2. Two Types: (a) Preservative FA — repetitious, low-level habits (routines, addictions). Circular mechanisms. (b) Propriate FA — higher-level, self-directed motives linked to proprium. Values, interests, life goals. Gives personality coherence. "Means becomes end" — e.g., started job for money, continues for satisfaction.
  3. 6 Attributes of Mature Personality: (1) Extension of self — involvement beyond self. (2) Warm human interaction — intimacy + compassion. (3) Emotional security — self-acceptance, frustration tolerance. (4) Realistic perception and skills. (5) Self-insight and humor — knowing oneself; humor without hostility. (6) Unifying philosophy of life — purpose, values integrating life.
  4. Criticism: Difficult to validate empirically. Motives don't always become autonomous. Freudians argue childhood motives persist.

Characteristics of Traits (Allport) / Common vs Unique Traits 6 / 3 MARKS

  1. Traits are real, more generalized than habits, dynamic/determinative, empirically established, relatively independent, not moral judgments, studied both idiographically and nomothetically. Inconsistent acts don't disprove trait existence.
  2. For 3-mark: Cardinal (single dominating trait, rare), Central (~5-10 core traits), Secondary (situation-specific). Common vs Individual — Common for nomothetic comparison; Individual for idiographic understanding.

5. Projective Techniques

Classification of Projective Techniques 10 / 6 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2025 (3m), Dec 2024, Jun 2022, Dec 2020, Dec 2016
  1. Definition: Projective techniques present ambiguous, unstructured stimuli to which individuals respond freely, "projecting" unconscious needs, conflicts, and personality dynamics. Coined by Lawrence Frank (1939). Based on Freud's projective hypothesis.
  2. Frank's Classification (1939 — 5 categories): (a) Constructive — build/create from unstructured materials (draw a person, Mosaic test). (b) Constitutive — impose structure on unstructured stimuli (Rorschach inkblots). (c) Cathartic — express emotions through play/drama (play therapy, psychodrama). (d) Interpretative — interpret ambiguous pictures (TAT, CAT, Rosenzweig P-F Study). (e) Refractive — personality revealed through changes in communication (handwriting analysis/graphology).
  3. Lindzay's Classification (1959 — 5 categories, more widely used): (a) Association — respond with first thought (Rorschach, word association). (b) Construction — create story/product (TAT, CAT, MAPS). (c) Completion — finish incomplete sentences/stories (Rotter ISB, Rosenzweig P-F). (d) Arrangement/Choice — select/arrange stimuli (Szondi test). (e) Expressive — draw/paint/role-play (Draw-A-Person, play techniques).
  4. Strengths: Indirect — bypasses conscious defenses; reveals unconscious; no "correct" answer reduces faking; cross-culturally useful; comprehensive personality picture.
  5. Weaknesses: Poor standardization; low inter-scorer reliability; subjective interpretation; limited validity evidence; time-consuming; requires extensive training; cultural bias; questionable scientific status.

Thematic Apperception Test (TAT) 6 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2025, Dec 2021, Jun 2013, Dec 2017 (3m)
  1. Developed by Henry Murray and Christiana Morgan (1935) at Harvard. Based on "apperception" — perceiving in terms of past experiences and present needs.
  2. Structure: 20 B&W cards depicting ambiguous interpersonal situations + 1 blank card for pure imaginative projection. Gender-specific sets.
  3. Administration: Client tells story for each card covering: what led to scene (past), what is happening (present), what characters feel/think, and outcome (future).
  4. "Hero" concept: Character subject identifies with. Analysis of hero's needs (n-Achievement, n-Affiliation, n-Power, n-Aggression) and "press" (environmental forces).
  5. Scoring: Murray's need-press system; Bellak's system — hero identification, themes, conflicts, defenses, interpersonal relations, outcome patterns.
  6. CAT (Children's Apperception Test, Bellak, 1949) — animal figures for children 3-10. Ranks 4th in usage (Watkins, 1991). Best as part of comprehensive battery.

Rorschach Inkblot Test 6 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2024
  1. Hermann Rorschach (1921), Swiss psychiatrist. 10 inkblots — 5 black/grey, 2 black/red, 3 multi-colored. Published in "Psychodiagnostik."
  2. 3 Administration Phases: (a) Performance Proper — present cards sequentially, "What might this be?" Record verbatim. (b) Inquiry — clarify determinants: "What made it look like that?" (c) Scoring using Exner's Comprehensive System (1969).
  3. Scoring categories: Location (W/D/Dd), Determinants (F=form, C=color, M=human movement, shading), Content (H=human, A=animal). Response Productivity Ratio (R): 15-30 = normal.
  4. Holtzman HIT (1961): Improved — 2 parallel forms, 45 cards, 22 variables, addresses psychometric weaknesses. Ranked 2nd in usage. Strong for thought disorder assessment.

6. Eysenck — Trait/Type Hierarchy (PEN Model)

Eysenck's Type/Trait Hierarchy (PEN Model) 10 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2019, Dec 2015, Dec 2012, Dec 2014, Jun 2013, Jun 2011
  1. 4-Level Hierarchical Structure: Level 1: Specific responses (individual behaviors). Level 2: Habitual responses (repeated similar behaviors). Level 3: Traits (intercorrelated habits — sociability, impulsivity). Level 4: Superfactors/Types (E, N, P — orthogonal, normally distributed). Built through second-order factor analysis.
  2. Three Superfactors (PEN): (a) Extraversion-Introversion (E): Sociable, lively, active, assertive vs reserved, quiet, introspective. (b) Neuroticism-Stability (N): Anxious, moody, depressed, guilty vs calm, even-tempered, stable. (c) Psychoticism (P) — added 1976: Cold, egocentric, impulsive, antisocial vs warm, empathic, cooperative. Each normally distributed, independent.
  3. Biological Basis: E → ARAS (Ascending Reticular Activating System) — introverts higher cortical arousal, more sensitive. N → limbic system/visceral brain — neurotics lower thresholds for emotional activation. P → testosterone + MAO (monoamine oxidase) — low MAO vulnerability marker.
  4. Yerkes-Dodson Law: Inverted-U arousal-performance. Introverts perform better under low stimulation; extraverts under high stimulation.
  5. Measurement: EPQ (90 items, E+N+P+Lie Scale). EPI earlier (only E+N). JEPQ for children 7-15. Lemon Drop Test (Corcoran, 1964): introverts secrete 2× more saliva.
  6. Psychopathology Link: High N + High I → anxiety disorders (dysthymia, OCD). High N + High E → psychopathic disorders. High P → psychotic disorders.

Eysenck's Hierarchical Taxonomy 6 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2023
  1. 4-level model: Specific responses → Habitual responses → Traits → Superfactors (E, N, P).
  2. Superfactors are orthogonal (independent), normally distributed, and have strong biological/genetic basis. ARAS for E, limbic system for N, testosterone/MAO for P.
  3. Derived through factor analysis — second-order analysis of intercorrelations among primary traits. Distinguishes approach from Guilford (who didn't reduce to superfactors) and Cattell (16 source traits vs Eysenck's 3 superfactors).

Arousal Theory (Eysenck) / Extraversion and Cortical Arousal 6 / 3 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2025, Dec 2018
  1. Eysenck proposed Extraversion-Introversion differences based on ARAS functioning — the brain's arousal center in the brainstem.
  2. Introverts: Higher baseline cortical arousal → more sensitive to external stimulation → seek lower stimulation environments. More conditionable. Extraverts: Lower baseline arousal → seek higher stimulation (social activity, excitement, risk). Less conditionable.
  3. Yerkes-Dodson Law: Inverted-U performance-arousal. Introverts reach optimal at lower stimulation; extraverts at higher.
  4. Lemon Drop Test evidence (Corcoran, 1964): introverts higher salivary response. EEG: introverts less alpha activity (higher arousal).

7. Guilford's Trait Theory

Guilford's Trait Theory of Personality 6 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2025, Jun 2025, Dec 2022, Jun 2021, Dec 2020, Jun 2020, Dec 2019, Dec 2018, Dec 2015, Dec 2013
  1. J.P. Guilford (1959, 1988) proposed a systematic, factor-analytic trait model with hierarchical organization.
  2. 7 Modalities of Traits: Morphological (body-related), Physiological, Need/Interest, Attitudinal, Temperamental, Aptitude, Pathological. Each contains multiple specific traits.
  3. Three Levels: (a) Hexis/Hexic (most specific, situation-tied). (b) Primary Traits (intermediate, factor-analytic). (c) Type Level (broadest, clusters of primary traits).
  4. GZTS (Guilford-Zimmerman Temperament Survey): 10 bipolar trait dimensions — General activity, Restraint, Ascendance, Sociability, Emotional stability, Objectivity, Friendliness, Thoughtfulness, Personal relations, Masculinity. 300 items (30 per trait), True/False.
  5. Structure of Intellect (SOI): 3 dimensions — Operations (5: cognition, memory, divergent/convergent production, evaluation) × Content (5: visual, auditory, symbolic, semantic, behavioral) × Products (6: units, classes, relations, systems, transformations, implications) = 150 factors. Primarily intelligence model but links to personality via aptitudes/temperament.
  6. Comparison with Eysenck: Both hierarchical and factor-analytic. Eysenck = 3 superfactors; Guilford = 10 primary traits, no higher reduction. Eysenck emphasizes biological basis more strongly.

8. Assessment — Technical Criteria

Discuss Reliability and Validity 6 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2023, Jun 2021
  1. Reliability — consistency/stability of measurement. Types: (a) Test-retest (same test, different times, r>.70). (b) Split-half (two halves correlated, Spearman-Brown). (c) Parallel/Alternate forms. (d) Inter-scorer/Inter-rater (critical for projective tests). (e) Internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha, KR-20).
  2. Validity — measures what it claims to. Types: (a) Content — items represent domain. (b) Criterion-related — concurrent (correlation with existing measure) and predictive (correlation with future outcome). (c) Construct — test measures theoretical construct. Campbell & Fiske (1959) Multitrait-Multimethod Matrix: convergent validity (high correlation with different measures of same trait) and discriminant validity (low correlation with different traits).
  3. Reliability necessary but not sufficient for validity. Can be reliable (consistent) without being valid, but cannot be valid without being reliable.

Criteria of Standardization / Technical Criteria in Testing 10 / 6 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2025, Jun 2018 (10m), Jun 2024 (6m), Dec 2019
  1. Standardization: Uniform procedures — fixed administration instructions, standardized materials, time limits, objective scoring. Ensures comparability across people and times.
  2. Norms: Raw scores → percentiles, standard scores (z-scores, T-scores, stanines). Representative normative sample (age, gender, education, region). Updated periodically.
  3. Reliability: Test-retest, split-half, parallel forms, inter-rater, internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha). r ≥ .70 for research; r ≥ .90 for clinical decisions.
  4. Validity: Content, criterion (concurrent + predictive), construct (convergent + discriminant). Campbell & Fiske MTMM matrix critical for construct validation.
  5. Item Analysis: Difficulty level, discrimination index, distractor analysis (MCQs). Pilot testing, revision, cross-validation.
  6. Practical considerations: Test length/time, cost, qualified administration, cultural fairness, ethical guidelines. For 10 marks, expand each criterion with examples.

History of Personality Assessment 10 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2023, Dec 2012
  1. Ancient: Chinese civil service exams (~1000 BC). Greek humoral typology (Hippocrates/Galen, ~190 AD) — Sanguine, Phlegmatic, Melancholic, Choleric.
  2. 19th Century: Wundt's lab (Leipzig, 1879). Galton — first rating scales, questionnaires, statistics. James McKeen Cattell — coined "mental test" (1890). Binet-Simon scale (1905).
  3. WWI Era (1914-1918): Army Alpha/Beta group intelligence tests. Woodworth Personal Data Sheet (1918) — first formal personality inventory (116 items, screening soldiers).
  4. 1920s-1930s: German assessment center — multiple methods. Rorschach inkblots (1921). TAT (Morgan & Murray, 1935). Allport's lexical study (1936) — 17,953 trait words.
  5. 1940s: MMPI (Hathaway & McKinley, 1943) — empirically keyed, clinical scales. OSS (1942) — comprehensive situational tests for operatives.
  6. 1950s-1970s: Factor-analytic inventories — 16PF (Cattell, 1949), EPI (Eysenck, 1964). Behavioral assessment emerges. Mischel (1968) challenges trait consistency.
  7. 1980s-Present: Big Five consensus — NEO-PI. Computerized testing, EMA (Ecological Momentary Assessment), implicit measures (IAT). Multicultural assessment emphasis.

9. Cattell's Trait Theory

Cattell's Trait Theory / Formula for Personality 10 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2023, Dec 2021, Jun 2018
  1. Approach: Inductive, empirical, data-driven. Personality = that which predicts what a person will do. Extensive factor analysis — "theory of traits."
  2. Specification Equation: R = f(S, P) — Response is function of Situation and Personality. Mathematical: R = S₁T₁ + S₂T₂ + SₙTₙ. Each trait weighted by relevance to situation.
  3. Three Data Sources: (a) L-data (Life record) — real-world behavior, ratings. (b) Q-data (Questionnaire) — self-report. (c) OT-data (Objective Test) — performance on behavioral measures.
  4. Surface vs Source Traits: Surface — observable clusters of correlated behaviors. Source — underlying unitary factors from factor analysis, better predictors. 16 source traits = 16PF. Surface trait "sociability" may come from source traits Warmth (A) and Social Boldness (H).
  5. Categories of Traits: (a) Common vs Unique. (b) Surface vs Source. (c) Constitutional (genetic — ergs) vs Environmental-Mold (learned — sentiments). (d) Ability vs Temperament vs Dynamic (motivational).
  6. Dynamic Traits: Ergs (10 innate drives: hunger, sex, gregariousness, curiosity, escape, pugnacity, acquisitiveness, self-assertion, narcissistic sex, parental protectiveness). Sentiments (learned patterns around objects — career, religion, self). SEMs (Socially Shaped Ergonomic Manifolds).
  7. MAVA (Multiple Abstract Variance Analysis): ~1/3 heredity, 2/3 environment. Syntality: group personality. 16 PF: 16 source traits as bipolar dimensions; 3 Global Factors (Introversion-Extraversion, Openness vs Tough-Mindedness, Self-Controlled vs Unrestrained).

Categories of Traits (Cattell) / Surface vs Source 6 / 3 MARKS

  1. Common vs Unique: Common — shared within culture. Unique — individual.
  2. Surface vs Source: Surface — observable clusters. Source — underlying factors, more fundamental. 16 source traits in 16PF.
  3. Constitutional vs Environmental-Mold: Constitutional (genetic origin, ergs). Environmental-mold (learned, social experience, sentiments). MAVA shows ~1/3 genetic, 2/3 environmental.
  4. Ability vs Temperament vs Dynamic: Ability (effectiveness reaching goals). Temperament (stylistic — emotionality, pace). Dynamic (motivational — ergs, sentiments, attitudes).
  5. For 3 marks: Surface traits (observable clusters) vs Source traits (underlying factors via factor analysis, 16 in 16PF). Source traits are better predictors.

10. Freud — Psychoanalytic Theory

Freud's Psychoanalytic Theory of Personality 10 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2022, Jun 2018, Jun 2014, Dec 2013, Jun 2011
  1. Topographical Model (Iceberg): Conscious (~10%) — current awareness. Preconscious (~10-15%) — accessible memories. Unconscious (~75-80%) — repressed memories, instincts, unresolved conflicts. The unconscious is the true driver of behavior.
  2. Structural Model: (a) Id — unconscious, pleasure principle, libido, Eros (life) and Thanatos (death) instincts. Primary process thinking. (b) Ego — reality principle, mediates Id-Superego-reality. Secondary process thinking. Executive. (c) Superego — develops ~age 5 (Oedipus resolution), internalized morals. Ego-ideal + Conscience.
  3. Defense Mechanisms: Ego's unconscious anxiety-management strategies. Repression, Denial, Projection, Reaction Formation, Rationalization, Displacement, Regression, Sublimation (most adaptive — channel impulses into socially acceptable activity).
  4. Psychosexual Stages: Oral (0-18mo) → Anal (18mo-3yr) → Phallic (3-6yr, Oedipus/Electra) → Latency (6-puberty) → Genital (puberty+). Fixation at any stage shapes adult personality.
  5. Evaluation: Pioneering — first comprehensive theory, highlighted unconscious, childhood. Criticized — lacks empirical testability, over-emphasizes sex, androcentric, deterministic, based on limited clinical sample.

Freud's Stages of Psychosexual Development 10 / 6 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2025 (10m), Dec 2017 (10m), Jun 2021 (6m), Jun 2015 (6m)
  1. Oral Stage (0-18 months): Erogenous zone = mouth. Pleasure from sucking, biting. Weaning = key conflict. Fixation → smoking, overeating, dependency (oral-passive) or sarcasm, aggression (oral-aggressive).
  2. Anal Stage (18mo-3 years): Erogenous zone = anus. Pleasure from retaining/expelling feces. Toilet training central. Fixation: anal-retentive (orderly, stingy, stubborn) or anal-expulsive (messy, generous, disorganized).
  3. Phallic Stage (3-6 years): Erogenous zone = genitals. Oedipus complex (boys: desire for mother, rivalry with father, castration anxiety → identification with father → superego formation). Electra complex (girls: penis envy, desire for father, identification with mother). Fixation → vanity, recklessness.
  4. Latency Stage (6-puberty): Libido dormant. Energy channeled into school, friendships, hobbies. Same-sex peer preference. Ego/defense development.
  5. Genital Stage (puberty+): Sexual urges reawaken. Shift to opposite-sex peers. Mature sexuality — genuine intimacy, caring. Successful resolution → well-adjusted adult capable of love and work.
  6. Evaluation: Insightful but lacks empirical evidence; over-emphasizes sexuality; based on clinical observation; ignores social factors beyond family.

Structural Model of Personality + Defense Mechanisms 6 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2024, Dec 2019, Dec 2015
  1. Id: Original, instinctual. Pleasure principle. Unconscious. Contains libido, life/death instincts. Immediate gratification (primary process).
  2. Ego: Reality principle. Mediates Id-Superego-world. Decision-making, problem-solving (secondary process). Develops first year.
  3. Superego: Moral arm. Develops ~age 5 via identification. Conscience (prohibitions) + Ego-ideal (aspirations).
  4. Defense mechanisms: Unconscious ego strategies. Examples: Denial (refusing reality), Projection (attributing own impulses to others), Repression (pushing into unconscious), Reaction formation (expressing opposite), Sublimation (most adaptive — channeling into acceptable activity).

Defense Mechanisms 3 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2023, Dec 2016
  1. Unconscious strategies by ego to protect against anxiety from Id-Superego conflict.
  2. Key types: Repression (pushing into unconscious), Projection (attributing to others), Rationalization (logical excuses), Reaction Formation (expressing opposite), Displacement (redirecting to safer target), Sublimation (channeling into socially acceptable — most adaptive).
  3. Normal in moderation; excessive use → neurotic/anxiety disorders.

11. Karen Horney

Karen Horney's Theory / Basic Anxiety & Neurotic Needs 10 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2025, Dec 2018, Jun 2015, Dec 2014
  1. Basic Anxiety: "The feeling of being isolated and helpless in a potentially hostile world." Origin: Basic Evil from parents (rejection, indifference, overprotection, inconsistent behavior) → Basic Hostility in child → repressed (child needs parents) → transformed into free-floating Basic Anxiety. Core of all neurosis.
  2. 10 Neurotic Needs: (1) Affection/approval. (2) A "partner" to take over. (3) Restricted life. (4) Power. (5) Exploiting others. (6) Social recognition/prestige. (7) Personal admiration. (8) Personal achievement. (9) Self-sufficiency/independence. (10) Perfection and unassailability.
  3. 3 Coping Strategies (Interpersonal Orientations): (a) Moving Toward People (Compliance): Needs 1,2,3. "If I give in, I shall not be hurt." Self-effacing. Need for affection, approval, dominant partner. (b) Moving Against People (Aggression): Needs 4,5,6,7,8. "If I have power, no one can hurt me." Expansive. Need for power, exploitation, prestige. (c) Moving Away (Withdrawal/Detachment): Needs 9,10. "If I withdraw, nothing can hurt me." Resignation. Need for self-sufficiency, perfection.
  4. Theory of Self: Real Self (authentic core) vs Despised Self vs Ideal Self (unrealistic perfection). "Tyranny of the Should" — internalized impossible demands. "Search for Glory" — pursuit of idealized self. Neurotic pride → self-hatred when standards unmet.
  5. Womb Envy: Counter to Freud's penis envy. Men envy women's motherhood/creativity capacity → compensate through achievement. Feminist contribution.
  6. Neo-Freudian: Agreed with unconscious but emphasized social/cultural factors over biological/sexual. Neurosis = disturbed human relationships. More optimistic — growth possible.

Neurotic Needs / Basic Anxiety 6 / 3 MARKS

  1. 10 neurotic needs — exaggerated, rigid versions of normal needs, driven by basic anxiety. For 3 marks: list 10 needs organized into 3 coping strategies. For 6 marks: detail needs + origins (basic evil → basic hostility → basic anxiety).
  2. Basic anxiety: pervasive feeling of being isolated/helpless in hostile world. Origin from parental "basic evil" → repressed hostility → free-floating anxiety. Foundation of all neurotic development.

12. Sullivan — Interpersonal Theory

Sullivan's Interpersonal Theory of Personality 10 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2024, Jun 2019, Dec 2012
  1. Core Premise: "Personality is the relatively enduring pattern of recurrent interpersonal situations which characterize a human life." Personality manifests only in interpersonal relationships. Rejects Freud's drive model for an interpersonal model.
  2. Dynamisms: Enduring energy transformations characterizing interpersonal relations. Self-system = most inclusive dynamism, develops to manage anxiety and maintain security.
  3. Three Levels of Cognition: (a) Prototaxic — discrete, unconnected sensations (infancy). Pre-logical. (b) Parataxic — causal connections between coincidental events. Parataxic distortion — perceiving present relationships through past templates. (c) Syntaxic — logical, consensually validated. Highest mode. Emerges with language.
  4. Self-System: Develops from "good-me" (reward-associated), "bad-me" (anxiety-associated), and "not-me" (intolerable anxiety, dissociated). Security operations (dissociation, selective inattention) protect but limit growth.
  5. Anxiety: Chief disruptive force. Transmitted from mother to infant through empathy ("mothering one"). Euphoria = absence of anxiety. Tension = deviation from euphoria.
  6. 7 Developmental Epochs: Infancy → Childhood → Juvenile → Preadolescence → Early Adolescence → Late Adolescence → Adulthood. Timing dictated by social environment. Preadolescence is critical — first genuine intimacy with "chum" (same-sex friend).

Sullivan's Developmental Epochs 6 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2023, Dec 2018, Dec 2017
  1. Infancy (0-18mo): Oral zone. Prototaxic mode. Tender cooperation with mothering one. Anxiety vs Euphoria established.
  2. Childhood (18mo-5yr): Language → parataxic mode. Reward/punishment learning. Personifications begin. Gender identity emerges.
  3. Juvenile Era (5-9yr): Peer relationships. Syntaxic mode develops. Competition, cooperation, compromise. Social subordination.
  4. Preadolescence (9-12yr) — CRITICAL: Intimate relationship with "chum" (same-sex peer). First genuine love/collaboration. Capacity for true intimacy developed. Corrects earlier parataxic distortions.
  5. Early Adolescence (12-14yr): Puberty. Lust dynamism emerges. Interest shifts opposite sex. Conflict intimacy vs lust.
  6. Late Adolescence (14-21yr): Integration intimacy + lust → stable romantic relationships. Self-system matures. Vocational/educational choices.
  7. Adulthood (21+): Mature interpersonal relations. Career, family. Syntaxic mode dominant. Key: Epochs socially determined (not biologically fixed).

Personifications / Developmental Epochs 3 MARKS

  1. Personifications: Mental images of self and others — Good-me (reward/approval), Bad-me (anxiety), Not-me (intolerable anxiety, dissociated). Internalized from early interpersonal experiences.
  2. Developmental Epochs: Sullivan's 7 stages — Infancy, Childhood, Juvenile, Preadolescence (critical for intimacy via "chum"), Early/Late Adolescence, Adulthood. Socially determined transitions.

13. Pavlov & Skinner — Learning Theories

Pavlov's Classical Conditioning / Learning Theory 10 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2024, Jun 2021
  1. Basic Paradigm: UCS (food) → UCR (salivation). CS (bell/tone) paired with UCS → CR (salivation to bell alone). Association between CS and UCS forms the basis.
  2. Key Concepts: (a) Acquisition: Gradual CR establishment through CS-UCS pairings. Optimal interval ~0.5s. (b) Generalization: CR to similar stimuli (gradient). (c) Discrimination: Learning to respond only to specific CS when reinforced. (d) Extinction: CR disappears when CS presented without UCS. (e) Spontaneous Recovery: Reappearance of extinguished CR after rest. (f) Higher-Order Conditioning: New neutral stimulus paired with established CS becomes CS itself.
  3. Experimental Neurosis (Shenger-Krestovnika, 1921): Dogs developed "neurotic" behavior (agitation, aggression) when unable to discriminate between progressively similar stimuli. Demonstrates conflict role in psychopathology.
  4. Personality Applications: Emotional conditioning (fear, phobias — Watson & Rayner's Little Albert, 1920). Systematic desensitization (Wolpe). Aversion therapy. Flooding/Implosion. Explains how environmental stimuli trigger emotional/behavioral reactions.
  5. Limitations: Reductionist — explains only simple respondent behaviors. Ignores cognitive mediation. Cannot explain acquisition of novel, complex behaviors.

Skinner's Learning Theory / Operant Conditioning 10 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2022, Jun 2012
  1. Two Types of Behavior: (a) Respondent — elicited by known stimuli (reflexes). (b) Operant — emitted voluntarily, operates on environment. Personality = collection of operants shaped by reinforcement history.
  2. Reinforcement (increases behavior): Positive (adding pleasant stimulus) and Negative (removing aversive stimulus — escape/avoidance). Punishment (decreases): Positive (adding aversive) and Negative (removing pleasant — time-out).
  3. Schedules of Reinforcement: Continuous (fast acquisition, fast extinction). Fixed Ratio (high rate, post-reinforcement pause). Variable Ratio (highest rate, most extinction-resistant — gambling). Fixed Interval (scalloped pattern). Variable Interval (steady moderate rate).
  4. Shaping: Successive approximation — reinforcing steps progressively closer to target. Superstitious Behavior: Non-contingent reinforcement → continued repetition without causal link. Token Economy: Application in institutions, classrooms.
  5. Criticisms: Chomsky (1959) — cannot explain language. Boulding (1984) — animal studies may not generalize. Ignores cognition/emotion. Not comprehensive. Deterministic.

Schedules of Reinforcement 6 / 3 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2023, Dec 2021, Jun 2019, Dec/June 2018, Dec 2016, Jun 2015
  1. Skinner identified reinforcement schedules determining rate, pattern, and persistence of operant behavior.
  2. Continuous: Every response reinforced. Fast acquisition, fastest extinction.
  3. Fixed Ratio (FR): After fixed number. High rate; post-reinforcement pause. E.g., piece-rate pay.
  4. Variable Ratio (VR): After unpredictable number. Highest, most consistent rate. MOST resistant to extinction. E.g., gambling.
  5. Fixed Interval (FI): First response after fixed time. Scalloped pattern. E.g., weekly salary.
  6. Variable Interval (VI): First response after unpredictable time. Steady, moderate rate. E.g., random pop quizzes.
  7. Extinction: Behavior decreases when reinforcement withdrawn. Spontaneous recovery = temporary reappearance.

Principles of Classical Conditioning / Generalization & Discrimination 6 MARKS

  1. Classical Conditioning: Pavlov's paradigm — UCS→UCR, CS+UCS pairing → CR. Key principles: Acquisition, Generalization (CR to similar stimuli), Discrimination (respond only to specific CS), Extinction, Spontaneous Recovery, Higher-Order Conditioning.
  2. Generalization: CR elicited by stimuli similar to CS. Gradient — more similar, stronger response. Adaptive — avoids learning separate responses for each variation.
  3. Discrimination: Learning to respond only to specific CS. Achieved through differential reinforcement. Pavlov's experimental neurosis — behavioral breakdown when discrimination too difficult.

14. Humanistic — Maslow & Rogers

Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs 6 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2024, Dec 2021, Jun 2016, Dec 2015
  1. Maslow (1943, 1954) — "Third Force" (vs psychoanalysis and behaviorism). Holistic, human-centered. Focus on potentials, not deficits.
  2. 5 Levels (Hierarchical Pyramid): (1) Physiological — food, water, sleep, sex. Most prepotent. (2) Safety — security, stability, protection from fear. (3) Belongingness & Love — affiliation, acceptance, intimacy. (4) Esteem — lower (respect of others: status, recognition) + higher (self-respect: confidence, mastery). (5) Self-Actualisation — "What a man can be, he must be." Fulfilling potential.
  3. D-Needs vs B-Needs: D-Needs (Deficiency — levels 1-4): from lack, reduce tension, homeostasis. B-Needs (Being/Growth — level 5): continuous striving, expressive, not deficiency-driven.
  4. Lower needs must be substantially satisfied before higher emerge (pre-potency principle). ~2% achieve self-actualization.

Characteristics of Self-Actualisers 6 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2025, Dec 2024, Dec 2022, Jun 2018
  1. Maslow studied exemplary individuals (Lincoln, Einstein, Eleanor Roosevelt) to identify characteristics of self-actualizing people.
  2. Key characteristics: (1) Efficient perception of reality. (2) Acceptance of self, others, nature. (3) Spontaneity, simplicity. (4) Problem-centered (not ego-centered). (5) Need for privacy. (6) Autonomy — independent of culture. (7) Freshness of appreciation. (8) Peak experiences — moments of intense joy, transcendence. (9) Gemeinschaftsgefühl — deep identification with humanity. (10) Deep (few) interpersonal relations. (11) Democratic character. (12) Discrimination means/ends. (13) Philosophical, unhostile humor. (14) Creativeness. (15) Resistance to enculturation.
  3. Imperfections: Not perfect — can be ruthless, experience realistic anxiety, have "metapathologies" (loss of meaning).

Carl Rogers' Person-Centered / Self Theory 10 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2025, Jun 2022, Dec 2020, Jun 2013, Dec 2012
  1. Self Theory: "Self" = organized, consistent set of perceptions about oneself. (a) Real Self — who I actually am. (b) Ideal Self — who I wish to be. Congruence (real ≈ ideal) = psychological health. Incongruence = anxiety, defensiveness.
  2. Phenomenological Approach: Each person's subjective reality is their reality. Behavior is understood from individual's internal frame of reference.
  3. Actualizing Tendency: Innate drive toward growth, fulfillment. "The organism has one basic tendency — to actualize, maintain, and enhance." Foundation of all motivation.
  4. Conditions of Worth: When significant others provide positive regard conditionally ("I love you IF..."), child internalizes conditions → incongruence. Unconditional Positive Regard = acceptance without conditions → healthy development.
  5. Fully Functioning Person (5 Characteristics): (1) Openness to experience. (2) Existential living. (3) Organismic trusting. (4) Experiential freedom. (5) Creativity.
  6. 3 Necessary Therapeutic Conditions: Genuineness, Unconditional positive regard, Empathic understanding. Both necessary AND sufficient. Q-Sort technique measures real-ideal discrepancy.

D-Needs vs B-Needs / The Self 6 / 3 MARKS

  1. D-Needs: First 4 Maslow levels — deficiency-based, tension-reducing, homeostasis. Satisfaction eliminates motivation.
  2. B-Needs: Self-actualization level — continuous growth, not deficiency-driven. Satisfaction increases motivation. Includes metaneeds: truth, beauty, justice.
  3. The Self (Rogers): Real Self (who one is) vs Ideal Self (who one wishes to be). Congruence = adjustment. Conditions of worth create incongruence. Unconditional positive regard fosters congruence.

15. Self-Report Inventories

Self-Report Personality Tests 10 / 6 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2025, Jun 2016, Jun 2015
  1. Definition: Standardized questionnaires — individuals report on their feelings, attitudes, beliefs, behaviors. Response alternatives fixed (True/False, Likert). Most common personality assessment method.
  2. Types: Social traits/adjustment, Pathological/Clinical (MMPI), Screening, Attitudes/interests/values, Normal-range (16PF, NEO-PI, EPQ, MBTI). First: Galton (1880) rating scales. First formal: Woodworth Personal Data Sheet (1918) — 116 items.
  3. Strengths: Objective scoring; easy/economic administration; greater reliability than projective techniques; multi-dimensional; normative data; standardized.
  4. Weaknesses: (a) Deliberate deception/Faking — faking good (social desirability), faking bad (malingering). (b) Response sets — acquiescence, social desirability (Marlowe-Crowne, 1964), extreme responding. (c) Self-deception — limited self-awareness. (d) Cultural bias.
  5. Methods to Overcome: Rapport building; forced-choice format; conceal test purpose; validity/verification keys (Lie scale in EPQ, F-scale in MMPI); correction keys; infrequency items; multi-method assessment; repeating items in different forms.

MMPI-2 / Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory 6 / 3 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2024 (3m), Jun 2020 (3m), Dec 2019 (3m)
  1. Hathaway & McKinley (1943). Empirically keyed — items selected by how well they discriminate between diagnostic groups and normals. Not face valid.
  2. MMPI-2: 567 True/False/Cannot Say statements. 10 Clinical Scales: Hypochondriasis (Hs), Depression (D), Hysteria (Hy), Psychopathic Deviate (Pd), Masculinity-Femininity (Mf), Paranoia (Pa), Psychasthenia (Pt), Schizophrenia (Sc), Hypomania (Ma), Social Introversion (Si).
  3. 8 Validity Scales: L (Lie), F (Infrequency), K (Correction), VRIN, TRIN (inconsistent responding), Fb, Fp, S. Most widely used personality inventory globally. 50+ languages.

NEO-Personality Inventory (NEO-PI/NEO-PI-R) 6 / 3 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2025, Dec 2024, Jun 2017, Dec 2019
  1. Costa & McCrae (1985, 1992). Measures Five-Factor Model (OCEAN): Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism.
  2. NEO-PI-R: 240 items — 5 domains × 6 facets × 8 items each. Facets provide detailed trait profiles within each domain. Self-report (Form S) and observer-rating (Form R).
  3. NEO-FFI: 60-item short form (12 items per domain). For research with time constraints.
  4. Widely researched, cross-culturally validated, stable across adult lifespan. Most widely used normal personality measure in research.

Faking and Measures to Overcome Faking in Inventories 6 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2025, Dec 2017, Dec 2018, Jun 2019
  1. Faking: Deliberate response distortion — faking good (social desirability), faking bad/Malingering (exaggerating problems). Response sets: Acquiescence (agreeing tendency), social desirability, extreme/midpoint responding.
  2. Methods to overcome: (a) Validity/Verification scales (L, F, K in MMPI; Lie scale in EPQ). (b) Forced-choice format (choose between equally desirable alternatives). (c) Rapport building to reduce motivation to fake. (d) Concealing test purpose — indirect items. (e) Correction keys for statistical adjustment. (f) Infrequency items (rarely endorsed, detect random responding). (g) Multi-method assessment — corroborate with behavioral data, other-report, interviews. (h) Repeating items in different forms to detect inconsistency.

16. Interview Method

Purpose and Types of Interview Method / Strengths & Weaknesses 10 / 6 / 3 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2025, Dec 2013, Dec 2017, Jun 2016, Dec 2014, Jun 2014, Jun 2013
  1. Definition: Face-to-face interaction gathering information about personality, behavior, attitudes, and life history through structured questioning and observation.
  2. Purpose: Information gathering; hypothesis generation/testing; rapport building; non-verbal behavior observation; clinical diagnosis; selection/placement.
  3. Types: (a) Structured — predetermined questions, fixed order. High reliability, less flexibility. (b) Unstructured — open-ended, conversational. Rich data, low reliability. (c) Semi-structured — guide with flexibility to probe. Best balance. (d) Stress Interview — deliberate pressure. (e) Panel Interview — multiple interviewers.
  4. Strengths: Flexible; clarify ambiguous responses; observe non-verbal behavior; build rapport; rich contextual data; suitable for illiterate populations; deep probing.
  5. Weaknesses: Subjective — interviewer bias (halo effect, stereotyping, personal equation); low inter-rater reliability (unstructured); time-consuming, expensive; social desirability/faking by interviewee; requires skilled interviewer; cross-cultural issues.
  6. For 3 marks: Strengths = flexibility, non-verbal observation, rich data. Weaknesses = subjectivity, low reliability, time/cost, faking vulnerability.

17. Big Five / Five-Factor Model

Five-Factor Model (Big Five) of Personality 10 / 6 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2024 (10m), Jun 2021 (10m), Jun 2016 (10m), Dec 2014 (10m), Jun 2011 (10m), Dec 2017 (6m), Jun 2015 (6m)
  1. OCEAN Model: Five broad dimensions from lexical approach (Goldberg, 1981) and questionnaire approach (McCrae & Costa, 1987).
  2. The Five Factors: (a) Openness (O): Curious, imaginative, creative, unconventional, aesthetic. Low: practical, conventional. (b) Conscientiousness (C): Organized, disciplined, achievement-oriented, reliable. Low: spontaneous, disorganized. (c) Extraversion (E): Sociable, energetic, assertive, excitement-seeking, positive emotionality. Low: reserved, solitary. (d) Agreeableness (A): Cooperative, trusting, compassionate, altruistic. Low: competitive, skeptical, antagonistic. (e) Neuroticism (N): Anxious, moody, emotionally unstable, self-conscious. Low: calm, stable, resilient.
  3. Characteristics: Dimensions (not types) — continuum. Stable across adulthood (rank-order ~.70). Heritable (~40-60%). Universal (50+ cultures). "Psychology of the stranger."
  4. Measurement: NEO-PI-R (240 items, 6 facets). BFI (44 items, John). IPIP (Goldberg, public domain).
  5. Five-Factor Theory (FFT, McCrae & Costa, 1996): Basic tendencies (genetic, stable) vs Characteristic adaptations (learned, changeable).
  6. Criticisms: Eysenck argues only 3 (PEN). Block (1995) — factor analysis issues. Some traits beyond Big Five (impulsivity, honesty-humility in HEXACO).

18. Neurophysiological Basis of Traits & Types

Neurophysiological Basis of Traits and Types 10 / 6 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2024, Dec 2022, Dec 2020, Dec 2017, Jun 2014, Jun 2013, Jun 2012
  1. E — ARAS (Ascending Reticular Activating System): Regulates cortical arousal. Introverts: higher baseline arousal → more sensitive → seek calmer environments. Extraverts: lower baseline → seek stimulation. Evidence: Lemon Drop Test (Corcoran, 1964 — introverts 2× saliva), EEG (introverts less alpha = higher arousal), Yerkes-Dodson Law (inverted-U).
  2. N — Limbic System (Visceral Brain): Hippocampus, amygdala, septum, hypothalamus. High N: lower thresholds for emotional activation, amygdala hyperreactivity to negative stimuli (fMRI). Greater physiological response (heart rate, GSR) to stress.
  3. P — Testosterone & MAO: Higher testosterone, lower MAO (monoamine oxidase). Low MAO = vulnerability marker for psychopathology. Males score higher on P (testosterone-linked).
  4. Alternative Models: Gray's Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory — BAS (reward sensitivity, linked to E/impulsivity), BIS (punishment sensitivity, linked to N), FFFS. Zuckerman's Sensation-Seeking — dopamine pathways, MAO correlation.
  5. General Evidence: Twin/adoption studies — heritability 40-60%. Cross-cultural consistency. Psychophysiological measures (GSR, HR, EEG, fMRI). For 10 marks: expand each brain system with research evidence.

19. Nomothetic vs Idiographic & Other Key Concepts

Nomothetic vs Idiographic Approach to Personality 6 / 3 MARKS

Asked: Dec 2024, Dec 2023, Jun 2018, Dec 2014, Dec 2020
  1. Nomothetic: Compares individuals on common dimensions/traits across groups. Seeks general laws. Quantitative, large samples, factor analysis. Example: Big Five. Merits: scientific, replicable, generalizable. Demerits: loses individual uniqueness; surface-level ("psychology of the stranger").
  2. Idiographic: Studies individual in depth as unique, integrated whole. Case studies, biography, clinical interviews. Example: Allport's personal dispositions, Freud's case studies. Merits: rich, deep understanding; captures complexity. Demerits: poor generalizability, subjective, hard to compare.
  3. Integration: Allport advocated both. Modern: nomothetic instruments (NEO-PI) interpreted idiographically (individual profile). Convergent operations.

Person-Situation Controversy 3 MARKS

  1. Triggered by Mischel (1968) — argued behavior inconsistent across situations; personality coefficients rarely exceed r=.30 ("personality coefficient ceiling").
  2. Counter: Aggregation solves issue (Epstein, 1979) — traits predict aggregated behavior well. Self-monitoring — some more consistent than others.
  3. Current consensus: Interactionism — Behavior = Person × Situation. Both stable traits and situations matter.

Type Theories — Sheldon Somatotypes / Ayurvedic Body Types 6 / 3 MARKS

Asked: Jun 2025 (3m), Dec 2021 (6m), Jun 2017 (3m), Jun 2016 (3m), Jun 2012 (10m), Jun 2013 (10m)
  1. Sheldon (1942) — Somatotypes (Constitutional Psychology): 3 body types on 1-7 scale. (a) Ectomorph: thin, fragile → cerebrotonic (artistic, introverted, inhibited). (b) Endomorph: round, soft → viscerotonic (relaxed, sociable, comfort-seeking). (c) Mesomorph: muscular, athletic → somatotonic (dominant, energetic, risk-taking). Weak methodology (Tylor, 1965).
  2. Ayurvedic Body Types (Doshas): (a) Vata: thin, restless, creative, anxious (ectomorph-like). (b) Pitta: medium, intense, ambitious, competitive (mesomorph-like). (c) Kapha: heavy, calm, loyal, slow (endomorph-like). Emerging in recent exams (post-2015).
  3. Introverts vs Extroverts (Jung): Introverts — inner-directed, reserved, prefer solitude, think before acting. Extroverts — outer-directed, sociable, active, think while speaking. Eysenck added biological basis (ARAS). MBTI: E/I as one dimension. Most frequent 3-mark short note.

Apperception / Purpose of Personality Assessment / Myers-Briggs Type Indicator 3 MARKS

  1. Apperception: Interpreting new experiences in light of past experiences and present needs. Key to TAT — subjects "apperceive" ambiguous stimuli through their personality dynamics.
  2. Purpose of Personality Assessment: (1) Identification/diagnosis of psychopathology. (2) Description of personality profile. (3) Prediction of future behavior/performance. (4) Treatment planning and evaluation. (5) Selection/placement decisions (employment, education). (6) Research on individual differences.
  3. Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI): Based on Jung's typology. 4 dimensions: Extraversion-Introversion, Sensing-Intuition, Thinking-Feeling, Judging-Perceiving → 16 personality types. Used for career counseling, team building. Isabel Myers & Katherine Briggs developed after WWII. Criticized for poor test-retest reliability and forced categorical classification.
