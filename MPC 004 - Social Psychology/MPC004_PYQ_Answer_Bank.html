MPC-004 · Advanced Social Psychology

Answer for marks.

High-yield answer frameworks built from repeated 2011–2025 PYQ clusters. Expand each point with examples and wording from your prescribed blocks in the exam.

For a 10-marker, use a short introduction, 4–6 labelled headings, one named theory or study, an illustration, and a conclusion. These are answer frameworks—not text to memorise word-for-word.

10 marks · Foundations

Nature and concept of social psychology

Opening: Social psychology is the scientific study of how people think about, influence and relate to one another in social contexts.

  1. Scientific discipline: uses systematic observation, hypotheses, measurement and research methods.
  2. Individual in context: explains behaviour through actual, imagined or implied presence of others.
  3. Core processes: social cognition, influence, attitudes, attraction, group processes, prejudice and conflict.
  4. Multiple levels: individual, interpersonal, group and societal levels of analysis complement one another.
  5. Applied value: informs health, law, education, organisations, media, community life and intergroup relations.

Close: The discipline is distinctive because it joins psychological processes with the social situations that activate, constrain or transform them.

10 marks · Social influence

Conformity and Asch’s studies

Definition: Conformity is a change in behaviour, judgement or belief brought about by real or imagined group pressure.

  1. Asch’s line-judgement paradigm: participants judged line lengths in a group containing confederates who gave a unanimous wrong answer.
  2. Key finding: many participants conformed at least once despite the answer being objectively clear.
  3. Normative influence: conformity to gain approval or avoid disapproval.
  4. Informational influence: conformity because others are treated as evidence about reality.
  5. Factors: unanimity, group size, ambiguity, task difficulty, status, culture and the presence of a dissenter.

Evaluation: Asch demonstrates social pressure clearly, but artificial tasks and cultural context limit direct generalisation to everyday life.

6 / 10 marks · Attitudes

Factors affecting attitude formation and change

  1. Learning: classical conditioning, operant conditioning and modelling attach evaluations to objects and groups.
  2. Family, peers and culture: socialisation provides norms, values and reference groups.
  3. Direct experience: personally experienced attitudes are often stronger and more accessible.
  4. Message factors: source credibility, quality of arguments, emotional appeal, repetition and channel matter.
  5. Recipient factors: involvement, prior knowledge, self-esteem and motivation influence persuasion.
  6. Cognitive consistency: imbalance or dissonance can motivate attitude or behaviour change.

Named theories: Heider explains balance; Katz explains attitude functions; Bem explains how people infer attitudes from their own behaviour.

10 marks · Prejudice

Prejudice, stereotypes and methods of reduction

Distinction: stereotype = belief; prejudice = attitude/evaluation; discrimination = unequal action or treatment.

  1. Origins: social categorisation, learned norms, realistic conflict, unequal status, scapegoating and cognitive shortcuts can contribute.
  2. Maintenance: selective attention, confirmation bias, segregation, institutional practices and self-fulfilling expectations preserve prejudice.
  3. Consequences: exclusion, lowered opportunity, stress, intergroup hostility and discriminatory behaviour.
  4. Contact: contact works best when groups have equal status, cooperation, common goals and institutional support.
  5. Recategorisation: build a shared superordinate identity rather than only separate group identities.
  6. Education and norms: challenge stereotypes, promote perspective taking and make anti-discrimination norms visible.

Close: Effective reduction combines individual attitude change with changes in social conditions and institutional norms.

10 marks · Prosocial behaviour

Prosocial behaviour and the empathy-altruism hypothesis

Definition: Prosocial behaviour is voluntary action intended to benefit another person; altruism refers to helping motivated primarily by concern for the other’s welfare.

  1. Helping decisions: notice the event, interpret it as an emergency, accept responsibility, know how to help and act.
  2. Situational factors: ambiguity, time pressure, number of bystanders, cost and danger affect helping.
  3. Personal and social factors: empathy, similarity, mood, modelling, norms of responsibility and reciprocity matter.
  4. Empathy-altruism: Batson proposed that empathic concern can create an altruistic, not merely self-interested, motive to help.
  5. Applications: reduce ambiguity, assign responsibility clearly, teach helping skills and model prosocial action.

Evaluation: Helping can have multiple motives; empathy is important but not the only explanation for prosocial action.

10 marks · Aggression

Nature, causes and reduction of aggression

Definition: Aggression is behaviour intended to harm another person who is motivated to avoid that harm. It may be physical, verbal, direct, indirect, hostile or instrumental.

  1. Biological/temperamental factors: arousal and individual differences may create vulnerability, but do not determine aggression alone.
  2. Frustration and aversive events: blocked goals, pain, heat and provocation can increase aggressive readiness.
  3. Social learning: models, observation and reinforcement teach aggressive scripts and expectations.
  4. Cognitive and situational cues: hostile attribution bias, weapons cues, alcohol and peer norms can intensify aggression.
  5. Reduction: reduce provocation, reward non-aggression, teach regulation and conflict skills, provide prosocial models and improve environments.

6 / 10 marks · Person perception

Attribution theory and errors in attribution

Attribution is the process of explaining behaviour through causes. Explanations may emphasise internal dispositions or external situations.

  1. Internal attribution: explains action through personality, ability, motives or effort.
  2. External attribution: explains action through task difficulty, roles, constraints, luck or social pressure.
  3. Fundamental attribution error: overestimates dispositions and underestimates situations for others.
  4. Actor-observer difference: actors often stress circumstances for their own behaviour, observers stress personal qualities.
  5. Self-serving bias: credit success internally while explaining failure through external conditions.

Use: Include an everyday illustration and note that attribution errors can feed stereotypes and interpersonal misunderstanding.

10 marks · Groups

Meaning, characteristics and formation of groups

Definition: A group consists of interdependent people who interact, recognise membership and are connected by shared goals, identity, roles or norms.

  1. Characteristics: interaction, interdependence, common goals, norms, roles, boundaries, structure and identity.
  2. Formation: proximity, similarity, shared needs, common goals, attraction, external threat and social identity can bring people together.
  3. Types: primary/secondary, formal/informal, in-group/out-group, reference groups and task groups.
  4. Development: Tuckman’s forming, storming, norming, performing and adjourning offers a useful sequence.
  5. Implications: cohesion and communication support performance, though excessive pressure for agreement may impair critical thinking.

6 / 10 marks · Conflict

Nature, forms and resolution of social conflict

  1. Meaning: conflict arises from perceived incompatibility of goals, values, interests or resources.
  2. Forms: interpersonal, intragroup, intergroup and societal conflict; conflicts may concern tasks, relationships or values.
  3. Causes: competition for scarce resources, misperception, unequal power, poor communication, identity threat and incompatible goals.
  4. Resolution: negotiation, mediation, cooperation, superordinate goals, perspective taking, communication and fair procedures.
  5. Constructive possibility: task disagreement can improve decisions when managed respectfully; hostile relationship conflict usually damages trust and performance.

6 / 10 marks · Crowd behaviour

Crowd behaviour and major theoretical approaches

Start: A crowd is a temporary gathering whose members are in relative proximity and share attention or action. Do not equate every crowd with irrationality.

  1. Contagion accounts: emotions and actions may spread rapidly through a crowd.
  2. Convergence: people with similar predispositions may gather, so the crowd does not create all behaviour from nothing.
  3. Emergent norm: new norms can develop in ambiguous situations and guide collective action.
  4. Social identity approach: crowd behaviour may be meaningful, patterned and shaped by shared group identity and relations with other groups.

Exam tip: Define active and inactive crowds if asked, then compare crowd behaviour with the wider category of collective behaviour.

10 / 6 marks · Research methods

Experimental, correlational and observational methods

Experimental method: the researcher manipulates an independent variable, controls relevant extraneous variables and measures a dependent variable. Its major strength is stronger causal inference; limitations include artificiality, demand characteristics and ethical constraints.

Correlational method: measures the relationship between variables without manipulation. It is useful where experiment is impractical or unethical, but correlation does not prove causation because of directionality and third-variable problems.

Observation: records naturally occurring behaviour systematically. It offers ecological value, but observer bias, reactivity, interpretation and consent must be managed.

Exam structure: define the method → explain procedure → strengths → limitations → one social-psychology use case.

10 / 6 marks · Ethics

Ethical issues in social psychological research

  1. Informed consent: participation should be voluntary and based on understandable information about procedures, risks and rights.
  2. Deception: use only when justified by scientific value, no viable alternative exists, and foreseeable harm is minimised.
  3. Protection from harm: anticipate psychological, social and physical risk; stop or modify procedures where distress becomes unacceptable.
  4. Privacy and confidentiality: protect identity, data and sensitive disclosures.
  5. Right to withdraw: participants must be free to leave without penalty.
  6. Debriefing: explain the study afterwards, especially where information was withheld or deception used.

Close: Ethics protects participants and also improves public trust in psychological knowledge.

6 / 10 marks · Compliance

Compliance: Cialdini’s principles and strategies

Compliance is behavioural agreement with a direct request. Cialdini’s commonly tested principles are reciprocation, commitment/consistency, social validation, liking, authority/credibility and scarcity.

  1. Foot-in-the-door: a small request precedes a larger related request.
  2. Door-in-the-face: an initially large request is followed by a smaller one after refusal.
  3. Low-ball: agreement is secured before costs are increased or advantages removed.

Use: Define the principle, state the mechanism, then give a plain everyday example. Do not confuse compliance with conformity or obedience.

10 / 6 marks · Obedience

Obedience and factors that increase it

Obedience is compliance with a command from a person perceived as legitimate authority. It differs from conformity because pressure comes from authority rather than a peer majority.

  1. Legitimacy and institutional setting: recognised authority and prestigious settings increase compliance.
  2. Gradual commitment: small initial acts can make escalation easier to accept.
  3. Proximity: obedience tends to increase with authority proximity and decrease with victim proximity.
  4. Diffusion of responsibility: people may see themselves as agents carrying out another’s wishes.
  5. Peer dissent: a disobedient peer can sharply reduce obedience.

Studies: use Milgram, Stanford Prison or Hofling only to illustrate the factor being explained; then mention ethical debate and limits.

6 / 10 marks · Attitude organisation

Heider’s balance theory, Katz’s functional theory and Bem’s self-perception theory

Heider: people prefer balanced relations among self, another person and an attitude object; imbalance creates pressure for attitude or relationship change.

Katz: attitudes can serve utilitarian, knowledge, value-expressive and ego-defensive functions. Messages persuade more effectively when they address the relevant function.

Bem: people may infer weak or unclear attitudes by observing their own behaviour and the conditions in which it occurs.

Comparison: Heider emphasises consistency, Katz emphasises purpose, and Bem emphasises inference from behaviour.

6 / 10 marks · Stereotypes & prejudice

Characteristics, development and manifestation of prejudice

  1. Cognitive component: categorisation and stereotypes simplify information but exaggerate between-group differences.
  2. Affective component: negative emotion or dislike can attach to a category.
  3. Behavioural component: prejudice may be expressed as avoidance, exclusion, hostility or discrimination.
  4. Development: family, peer, media and cultural norms transmit group evaluations; unequal status and competition can reinforce them.
  5. Maintenance: confirmation bias, selective attention, segregation and self-fulfilling expectations make stereotypes resistant to change.

Answer guardrail: define stereotype, prejudice and discrimination separately before explaining how they connect.

6 / 10 marks · Interpersonal attraction

Factors affecting interpersonal attraction

  1. Proximity and exposure: repeated contact increases familiarity and opportunity for interaction.
  2. Similarity: shared attitudes, values and interests validate beliefs and ease interaction.
  3. Reciprocal liking: being liked is rewarding and signals acceptance.
  4. Physical attractiveness: affects first impressions, though its influence depends on context and social standards.
  5. Competence and self-disclosure: ability can attract when combined with warmth; appropriate disclosure builds intimacy.

Close: Attraction is not explained by one factor; it emerges from opportunities, perceived rewards, similarity and social context.

6 / 10 marks · Cooperation

Cooperation and competition as social processes

Cooperation is coordinated action toward shared or compatible goals. Competition involves pursuing outcomes that are scarce or mutually exclusive.

  1. Conditions supporting cooperation: interdependence, communication, trust, equitable rules, shared identity and superordinate goals.
  2. Conditions supporting competition: scarce resources, zero-sum framing, hostile norms, unequal power and intergroup comparison.
  3. Outcomes: cooperation can improve coordination and collective welfare; competition can motivate performance but may also produce conflict and exclusion.

PYQ connection: Link cooperation to prosocial behaviour and conflict resolution where the question asks for application.

6 / 10 marks · Group dynamics

Meaning, importance and stages of group dynamics

Group dynamics refers to the processes through which group members interact, influence one another, develop norms, communicate, make decisions and manage roles or conflict.

  1. Formation: members orient to task, membership and expectations.
  2. Storming: differences, power negotiation and conflict may emerge.
  3. Norming: shared standards, roles and cohesion become clearer.
  4. Performing: the group directs energy toward task accomplishment.
  5. Adjourning: temporary groups close, evaluate and separate.

Importance: Group dynamics explains performance, cohesion, leadership, communication, conflict and member satisfaction.

3 / 6 marks · Social identity

Social identity theory and in-group bias

Tajfel and Turner argued that part of self-concept comes from social group memberships. People categorise self and others, identify with in-groups and compare groups in ways that can favour the in-group. This helps explain group-based perception, prejudice and intergroup competition, while not implying that bias is inevitable in every group context.
