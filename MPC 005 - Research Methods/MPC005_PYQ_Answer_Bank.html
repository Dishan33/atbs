10 MARKS · FOUNDATIONS

Explain the steps in the research process.

Recurring long answer: 2011, 2013, 2015, 2017, 2019, 2023–25

Opening

Define research as a systematic, objective and empirical process for answering questions. In psychology, controlled observation and evidence make it possible to develop, test and refine concepts, principles and explanations of behaviour. State that the sequence may be iterative but each step makes the final conclusion more defensible.

Answer structure

  1. Identify and delimit the problem: turn an area of interest into a feasible question.
  2. Review literature and frame concepts: locate theory, evidence and the knowledge gap.
  3. Formulate hypothesis/questions: state a clear, testable prediction where appropriate.
  4. Identify and operationalise variables: decide how constructs will be measured/manipulated.
  5. Choose design and sample: match method to question and specify population/selection.
  6. Collect data ethically: use standardised procedures, consent and safeguards against bias.
  7. Analyse and interpret: apply suitable analysis; distinguish finding from inference.
  8. Conclude, report and publish: state implications, limitations and future directions.

Close

Conclude that a valid research conclusion depends on the coherence of the whole chain, not on data analysis alone.

10 MARKS · MEASUREMENT

Define reliability. Describe methods of estimating reliability.

Recurring: 2012, 2013, 2015, 2017–18, 2021–25

Opening

Reliability is the consistency or dependability of measurement. Explain that a reliable tool reduces random measurement error, but reliability alone does not establish validity.

Methods

  • Test-retest: administer the same test twice and correlate scores; estimates stability over time.
  • Parallel/equivalent forms: correlate scores from two comparable forms; reduces recall but requires equivalent forms.
  • Split-half: correlate two halves of a test and adjust the coefficient; an internal-consistency estimate.
  • Internal consistency: assess how consistently items measure the same construct (e.g., item consistency).
  • Inter-rater reliability: estimate agreement among independent observers/scorers.

Close

Name the method that fits the instrument and add that a reliability estimate must be interpreted alongside evidence of validity.

10 MARKS · VALIDITY

Explain threats to internal and external validity.

2011–14, 2015–16, 2018–20, 2021–25

Opening

Internal validity asks whether the IV caused the outcome; external validity asks whether findings generalise beyond the study.

Internal threats

Explain history, maturation, testing, instrumentation, regression, selection and attrition. For each, show how an alternative explanation could mimic a treatment effect.

External threats

Discuss unrepresentative samples, artificial settings/reactivity, interaction of selection and treatment, and time/context specificity. Use cautious language: generalisation is supported by converging replication, not assumed.

Types of validity

For a “types” question, add content validity (adequate coverage of the domain), criterion-related validity (relation with an appropriate criterion) and construct validity. Construct evidence includes convergent validity—the measure relates to measures it should relate to—and discriminant validity—it does not relate too strongly to distinct constructs.

Close

State that good design balances control with realism and reports limits rather than hiding them.

6 MARKS · VARIABLES

Differentiate variables and constructs. Explain types of variables.

2013, 2015–16, 2021–23, 2025

Framework

A construct is an abstract theoretical concept (e.g., anxiety); a variable is an observable or measurable characteristic that can vary (e.g., anxiety score). Then organise types:

  • Independent and dependent variables
  • Active/manipulable and attribute/pre-existing variables
  • Extraneous variables and the special case of confounded variables
  • Continuous and discrete variables where relevant

For a construct-focused PYQ, add that a construct is a concept adopted for a scientific purpose. Intervening variables summarise an inferred internal process, whereas a hypothetical construct is a theoretical term used to describe something regarded as real but not directly observed.

Use one matched psychological example and end by noting that operational definitions link constructs to variables.

10 MARKS · SAMPLING

Discuss probability and non-probability sampling methods.

2012, 2015, 2018, 2020, 2023, 2025

Opening

Define population, sample and sampling. The purpose is to obtain evidence about a population while managing feasibility and bias.

Organise by selection principle

  • Probability: simple random, systematic, stratified and cluster sampling. Each member has a known chance of selection.
  • Non-probability: convenience, purposive, quota and snowball sampling. Selection probability is unknown.

Evaluation

Probability sampling generally supports stronger statistical generalisation but needs a sampling frame and resources. Non-probability methods can be appropriate for exploratory or hard-to-reach groups, but selection bias must be acknowledged. For a comparison question, define purposive selection by relevant characteristics and snowball selection through participant referrals.

10 MARKS · SURVEY

Describe types of survey research and the steps involved.

2012, 2016, 2019, 2023–25

Opening

Survey research is a descriptive, non-experimental approach using standardised questions or interviews to gather information from a sample.

Types

Contrast cross-sectional surveys (one point in time) with longitudinal surveys: trend, cohort and panel. Mention that surveys may use questionnaires, interviews, telephone or online modes. For difficulties/issues, note non-response, inaccurate self-report, social desirability, recall limits, wording effects, cost and follow-up loss in longitudinal work.

Steps

  1. Set focused objectives and define population.
  2. Design questions/instrument; avoid leading or double-barrelled items.
  3. Pilot and revise.
  4. Select sample.
  5. Administer consistently and protect confidentiality.
  6. Code, analyse, interpret and report limits such as non-response.
10 MARKS · EX-POST FACTO

Differentiate ex-post facto and experimental research. Describe ex-post facto steps.

2012–13, 2016–17, 2019–20, 2021–25

Contrast first

In an experiment, the researcher manipulates the IV and uses control/random assignment where possible. In ex-post facto research, the presumed cause or group difference already exists and cannot be manipulated.

Steps

  1. State problem and proposed relationship.
  2. Identify criterion and relevant naturally occurring groups.
  3. Select comparable groups; match/control plausible extraneous variables.
  4. Gather existing or newly measured evidence.
  5. Compare outcomes and interpret cautiously.

Evaluation

It is useful where manipulation is impossible or unethical, but alternative explanations limit causal claims.

6 MARKS · FIELD EXPERIMENT

Explain field experiment: strengths, weaknesses and planning.

2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, 2020–25

Framework

Define it as an experiment conducted in a natural setting. Explain planning through a clear IV/DV, feasible comparison/control, standardised procedure, ethical safeguards and a plan for managing situational variation.

  • Strengths: natural behaviour and ecological validity.
  • Weaknesses: reduced control, difficult random assignment/replication, practical and ethical constraints.

Finish by contrasting it with a laboratory experiment, not with a non-experimental field observation.

10 MARKS · CASE STUDY

Explain case study as a research method.

2014–18, 2023–24

Opening

A case study is an intensive contextual investigation of a bounded individual, group, event, institution or community.

Body

Cover case selection, defining the case and context, gathering multiple sources (interviews, observation, records/documents), triangulating evidence, analysing patterns and presenting a rich account. For selection, use information-oriented cases: critical, extreme/deviant or paradigmatic cases can be more informative than an average case. Explain types where relevant: illustrative, exploratory, cumulative or critical-instance case study.

Evaluation

Its strength is depth and holistic understanding; its limitation is that findings may not generalise automatically. The answer should reject the misconceptions that a case study is merely anecdotal, only clinical, or “unscientific” simply because it uses a small N. Its generalisation is analytical and evidence-based, not automatic statistical generalisation.

10 MARKS · SINGLE-FACTOR

Explain single-factor design with suitable examples.

2013–14, 2016–17, 2021–23, 2025

Core definition

A single-factor design has one independent variable with two or more levels. Define factor, levels, IV and DV before describing forms.

Forms

  • Between-group: different people receive each level; avoids carryover but individual differences can add error.
  • Within-subject: same people experience all levels; controls individual differences but needs counterbalancing to address order/fatigue effects.
  • Randomised-group design: participants are randomly assigned to separate conditions. Random assignment helps distribute individual differences, but it does not by itself remove every contextual or measurement threat.

Example

Compare recall after silence, instrumental music and lyrical music. State the factor, its three levels and the memory-score DV.

10 MARKS · FACTORIAL DESIGN

Explain main and interaction effects in factorial design with an example.

2011–12, 2014–15, 2018, 2020–21, 2023–25

Core definition

A factorial design includes two or more factors and permits simultaneous study of each factor and their joint effect. Define the factor, its levels, the resulting cells, and the dependent variable before explaining the notation.

Explain with 2 × 2

Use study environment (quiet/noisy) × sleep (adequate/restricted) on memory score. A main effect is the overall effect of one factor averaged across the other. An interaction occurs if the effect of noise is much larger after restricted sleep than after adequate sleep.

Close

State why factorial designs are efficient: they test interactions that separate single-factor studies cannot reveal. They may be between-subject, within-subject or mixed; their cost is the larger number of conditions and more complex interpretation.

10 MARKS · QUASI-EXPERIMENT

Explain types of quasi-experimental design and evaluate them.

2011–13, 2015–16, 2018, 2021, 2024–25

Opening

Quasi-experimental designs evaluate interventions without full random assignment and/or full experimental control.

Types

  • One-group pretest-posttest design
  • Non-equivalent control-group design
  • Separate pretest–posttest samples, double-pretest and switching-replications designs
  • Interrupted/multiple time-series, repeated-treatment and counterbalanced designs

Evaluation

They are valuable in natural institutions and where randomisation is impossible, but selection, history and maturation threaten internal validity. Strengthen the design through comparison groups, repeated observations and transparent limits.

6 MARKS · CORRELATION

Discuss advantages and limitations of correlational design.

2012–18, 2020–21, 2023–24

Framework

Define correlational design as measurement of covariation between variables without manipulation.

For a “types” question, distinguish bivariate correlation (two variables), multiple correlation (several predictors) and partial correlation (the association after controlling specified variables). The coefficient expresses direction and degree, not a causal mechanism.

  • Advantages: studies naturally occurring variables; permits prediction; useful when manipulation is unethical/impossible; can reveal strength/direction of association.
  • Limitations: correlation does not prove causation because of directionality and third-variable problems.

Close with an example: stress and sleep may correlate, but neither variable can be treated as the proven cause from correlation alone.

10 MARKS · QUALITATIVE RESEARCH

Differentiate qualitative and quantitative research. Explain types of qualitative research.

2011–12, 2016, 2019–20, 2022, 2024–25

Comparison

Compare purpose (meaning/context vs measurement/testing), data (textual/observational vs numerical), sampling (purposive/theoretical vs often probability), analysis (interpretive vs statistical) and quality criteria (credibility etc. vs reliability/validity).

Types

Explain case study, ethnography, phenomenology, grounded theory, historical method and discourse analysis. Give one line on the question each is suited to.

Close

Do not describe one as superior: they answer different questions and can complement one another.

10 MARKS · ETHNOGRAPHY

Explain the assumptions and steps in ethnography.

2012, 2015–17, 2019, 2022–25

Opening

Ethnography studies a culture, community or social world in its natural context, seeking participants’ meanings and practices.

Assumptions

Meaning is contextual; culture is learned and shared; participants’ perspectives matter; the researcher is part of the interpretive process; prolonged engagement supports understanding.

Steps

Select field/question; negotiate access and ethics; build rapport; observe/participate and record field notes; conduct interviews and collect documents; analyse recurring cultural patterns; check/refine interpretations; report reflexively and protect identities.

10 MARKS · GROUNDED THEORY

Explain goals, steps and coding in grounded theory.

2011–12, 2015–18, 2021–25

Opening

Grounded theory aims to generate a theory that is grounded in systematically collected and analysed data rather than imposed in advance.

Process

  1. Collect initial data and begin analysis immediately.
  2. Use constant comparison across incidents, participants and codes.
  3. Open coding: identify concepts and labels.
  4. Axial coding: relate categories, conditions and consequences.
  5. Selective coding: integrate around a core category.
  6. Use theoretical sampling and memo writing until categories are sufficiently developed.

Close

Emphasise that theory emerges through disciplined comparison, not through merely listing interview themes.

10 MARKS · DISCOURSE ANALYSIS

Explain approaches and steps in discourse analysis.

2011–12, 2015–18, 2021–25

Opening

Discourse analysis studies how language in talk or text constructs social reality, identities, actions and power. Language is treated as doing something, not simply reporting facts.

Steps

  1. Define a focused question and select a relevant corpus.
  2. Transcribe/familiarise with the material and its context.
  3. Identify recurring repertoires, categories, rhetorical devices or patterns.
  4. Examine what versions of reality are built and what functions they serve.
  5. Relate interpretation to power, institutions or interaction where appropriate.
  6. Support claims with carefully chosen extracts and reflexive commentary.

Close

Distinguish it from content analysis: discourse analysis attends to how language works, not only to topic frequency.

6 MARKS · QUALITATIVE ANALYSIS

Describe steps in analysing and evaluating qualitative data.

2011, 2013–15, 2018–21, 2023–25

Framework

  1. Organise/transcribe data and become familiar with it.
  2. Code meaningful segments.
  3. Group codes into categories or themes and compare cases.
  4. Interpret patterns in context, using data extracts as evidence.
  5. Evaluate credibility, dependability, confirmability and transferability through transparency, reflexivity, audit trails, triangulation or member checking where suitable.

End by stating that interpretation must be traceable to evidence, not just persuasive prose.

6 MARKS · QUALITATIVE REPORT

Discuss the contents of a qualitative research report.

2011, 2014, 2016–17, 2021, 2023–24

Framework

Organise the report through: title/abstract; background and research question; methodological approach and researcher position; participants/setting and ethics; data collection/analysis procedure; findings supported by extracts; interpretation/discussion; limitations, implications and references. Explain that reflexivity and anonymisation are especially important in qualitative reporting. In interpretation, separate what the data show from the researcher’s inference, acknowledge alternative explanations and limits, and do not claim more generalisation than the design supports.

3 MARKS · RESEARCH SAFEGUARDS

Write a short note on objectivity safeguards in research.

Asked in short-answer form: 2023 and 2025

Framework

Define objectivity as minimising the influence of personal preference on research decisions. Give three concise safeguards: operational definitions and standardised procedures; controls, randomisation or blind assessment; and transparent records, independent checking or peer review. Conclude that safeguards reduce bias but do not eliminate the need for reflexive ethical judgement.

10 MARKS · DESIGN

Explain research design and the main features of true experimental research.

PYQ family: research design, objectives of design, experimental research and experimental designs

Opening

Research design is the plan, structure and strategy of investigation. It specifies how variables will be operationalised, how a sample and data will be obtained, and how the hypothesis will be analysed so that the research question can be answered.

Objectives of a good design

  1. Answer the research question: produce valid, objective, accurate and economical evidence relevant to the hypothesis.
  2. Control variance: maximise systematic or experimental variance, control extraneous variance through elimination, randomisation or building a variable into the design, and minimise error variance through reliable measurement and controlled conditions.

True experimental research

  • Manipulation: the researcher deliberately varies an independent variable.
  • Control: relevant extraneous conditions are held constant or controlled, usually with a comparison/control condition.
  • Random assignment: participants are assigned to treatment conditions by chance, reducing systematic pre-existing differences.
  • Measurement of the dependent variable: the outcome is recorded after the planned treatment conditions, allowing a stronger causal inference than non-experimental designs.

Design forms to name

Use the form that fits the question: between-subjects, within-subjects/repeated measures, mixed, classical pretest–posttest control-group, Solomon four-group or factorial design. The final sentence should state that even a true experiment needs reliable measures, ethical procedures and a design appropriate to the hypothesis.

10 MARKS · HYPOTHESIS

Define a hypothesis. Explain its characteristics, formulation and types.

PYQ family: hypothesis formulation, null hypothesis and alternative hypothesis

Opening

A hypothesis is a tentative and testable statement about a possible relationship between two or more variables. It offers a potential answer to a solvable research problem and directs collection and interpretation of data.

Characteristics of a useful hypothesis

  • It is conceptually clear, specific and expressed in a form that can be empirically tested.
  • It is logically connected to the research problem and the existing body of theory or evidence.
  • It is capable of verification and operationalisation: the relevant variables and their measurement can be specified.

Formulation

Move from a defined problem and review of relevant theory/evidence to a clear statement of the expected relationship. Identify the variables, make their operational implications explicit, and choose a design and data-collection procedure capable of testing the statement. A hypothesis suggested after inspecting data should be tested on a new set of data rather than treated as confirmation.

Types and caution

A null hypothesis states no difference or relationship of scientific interest. An alternative hypothesis is the research hypothesis that states the relationship or difference the researcher expects. Lack of an adequate theoretical framework, insufficient knowledge of prior evidence and limited knowledge of scientific technique can make formulation difficult.

6 MARKS · OBJECTIVITY

Explain research bias and safeguards for objectivity.

Linked short-answer and research-quality PYQ family

Framework

Research bias is a systematic influence that can distort the question, data collection, analysis or interpretation. Personal expectations, observer effects, inconsistent administration and selective interpretation can make a conclusion look stronger than the evidence permits.

  • Specify operations and standardise procedures: define constructs, instructions, scoring and recording rules before collecting data.
  • Use appropriate control: random assignment, control/comparison conditions, counterbalancing or blind assessment where the design permits.
  • Strengthen measurement and checking: use reliable instruments, independent scoring/checking, transparent records and clear reporting of limitations.
  • Use reflexivity in qualitative work: state the researcher’s position and show how interpretations were checked against the data.
  • Name common biases when asked: a placebo effect can change responses because participants expect an effect; a post hoc fallacy mistakes an event following another for proof that it was caused by it.

Conclude that objectivity is strengthened through disciplined design and transparent evidence; it is not achieved by simply claiming neutrality.

10 MARKS · COMPARATIVE DESIGN

Explain causal-comparative research design and evaluate it against experimental research.

PYQ family: causal-comparative design, comparison with experimental/correlational design and evaluation

Opening

Causal-comparative research is a non-experimental design in which the researcher compares pre-existing groups to understand an existing difference on a variable of interest. The independent variable has already occurred or cannot be ethically/practically manipulated.

Basic procedure

  1. Select a group possessing the relevant attribute or condition and a comparison group without it.
  2. Define and measure the dependent variable with the same procedure for both groups.
  3. Compare the groups and consider plausible extraneous or confounding variables before interpreting the difference.

Comparison and evaluation

  • Unlike a true experiment, it has no researcher manipulation and no random assignment; therefore causal inference is more limited.
  • Unlike correlational design, it focuses on differences between groups; correlational research examines relationships among variables within a group.
  • It is useful where manipulation is impossible or unethical, but selection and pre-existing group differences threaten internal validity. Conclude cautiously: the design can suggest a causal explanation, not prove it as strongly as a controlled experiment.
6 MARKS · QUASI / FACTORIAL

Write notes on mixed factorial and interrupted time-series designs.

PYQ family: mixed factorial design and interrupted time-series design

Mixed factorial design

A mixed factorial design has at least one within-subject variable and at least one between-subject variable. For example, gender may be a between-subject variable while the same participants are measured under more than one treatment condition. It permits examination of main effects and interaction effects, while requiring attention to order effects for the repeated condition.

Interrupted time-series design

An interrupted time-series design compares a single group over time by examining the data trend before and after an intervention or event. Repeated observations make it possible to see whether the level or direction of the trend changes at the interruption. It is especially useful where random assignment is not feasible, but history and other concurrent changes must be considered before attributing the shift to the intervention.

6 MARKS · SURVEY

Explain the construction of questions for a survey instrument.

PYQ family: survey methods, survey instrument and types of survey questions

Framework

First decide the content, scope and objective of every question. The wording must be clear enough to elicit the information required by the research question, rather than merely sounding formal.

  • Use short, specific language appropriate to the respondent; avoid ambiguity, double-barrelled wording, loaded/leading wording and unnecessary assumptions.
  • Select a response format that fits the information required: open-ended responses, fixed alternatives, rating scales or Likert-type formats.
  • Arrange questions in a logical order, give understandable instructions, and avoid asking sensitive or demanding questions before rapport is established.
  • Pilot the instrument, check whether respondents understand it as intended, and revise wording, order or response options before the main survey.

End by linking question construction to validity: poorly worded questions create measurement error even when the sample is large.

6 MARKS · QUALITATIVE ANALYSIS

Differentiate content analysis and critical discourse analysis.

PYQ family: discourse analysis, critical discourse analysis and qualitative analysis

Content analysis

Content analysis is a systematic method for organising material into categories and examining what is represented in documents, media, responses or other text. The researcher defines the unit of analysis, develops categories, codes the material consistently and interprets patterns. It may attend to manifest content as well as more interpretive latent content.

Critical discourse analysis

Critical discourse analysis examines how language constructs meanings, social positions and relations of power in a particular context. It asks how vocabulary, framing, assumptions and interactional patterns work, rather than only how often a category occurs.

Close

Both require a transparent analytic procedure and examples from the material. Content analysis foregrounds systematic categorisation; critical discourse analysis foregrounds language, context and power.
